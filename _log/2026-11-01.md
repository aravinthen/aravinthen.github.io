## Game theory
1. I want to get to a point where I know enough about this field to talk to experts about it. I've already spent some time on game theory, but now I'd like to study it properly. 
2. Went through the first lecture of Roughgarden's notes on Algorithmic Game Theory. Some bangers:
    * "The next time we bemoan people exploiting loopholes to subvert the intent of the rule makers, instead of asking ’What’s wrong with these people?” let’s instead ask, 'What’s wrong with the rules?' and then adopt a scientifically principled approach to fixing them." - Hartline and Kleinberg.
    * The entire structure of the 2012 Olympic Badminton tournament is a really nice, pertinent example as to how optimizers might exploit tournament structures when they have outside knowledge (for example, the strength of another team). A good solution would have mechanisms that hide this information from the teams that compete: One simple example is that the players finish their group tournaments, are reduced to half the number and then are *randomly* assigned their opponents from a competing groups best performer or second best performer.
3. Mechanism design: the science of rule-making.
4. Interestingly, Roughgarden explicitly seeks out scenarios where selfish behaviour is benign. This is exactly the kind of thing I'm interested in form an alignment perspective, although for me I'm as interested in greed as I am in malformed incentives. Greed is merely one way an AI agent can go rogue.
5. **Price of anarchy**: the cost that emerges when participants in a system act selfishly rather than cooperatively. A POA that is close to 1 is a system where selfish behaviour leads to a near optimal outcome.
6. **Braess's paradox**, where the addition of a helpful resource or mechanism *actually makes the system less efficient overall*. There are even physical examples that demonstrate this!
7. Key results on Nash Equilibrium:
    1. With randomization, every bimatrix game has at least one Nash Equilibrium, 
    2. If the bimatrix game is zero-sum, that Nash equilibrium is computable in polynomial time.
    3. It is the above two points that give Nash equilibria as good predictors of behaviour in zero-sum games.
    4. There is no polynomial-time algorithm for computing Nash equilibria in general (non-zero-sum) games: this is a PPAD-hard problem, which is somewhat weaker as a measure of intractability than NP-hard. Roughgarden calls is a fundamental computational problem of 'intermediate' difficulty. Computer scientists are *amazing*, man.
