# Notes on Technical AI Safety 4 - The Heart of the Beast
We're onto one of the meatier fields of the AI alignment space: that of *mechanistic interpretability*. This is the neuroscience equivalent of the AI brain- **holy fucking shit I just realised that this is what Susan Calvin from I, Robot did for her job. That is awesome**. Goddamn, guys, we're at a point where we can actually study robopsychology. Give me a moment to let this sink in.

Anyway, *robopsychology* appeared to be a field of research that blended mathematics (with the laws of robotics forming something of a set of specific equations) and psychological methods assigned to robots. There was something clean and 'physico-mathematical' about that formulation of robot psychology, where alignment was merely a solution to a set of equations. 

Asimov viewed robots and robot intelligence as systems that operated via clean mathematical logic. Asimov had enormous faith in logic as a paradigm: there's a scene in the first Foundation book where a character uses symbolic logic to demonstrate that two days of conversations amounted to nothing of consequence.

Just like how nobody wants to decode two days of conversation into symbolic logic, nobody ~~wants to~~ currently uses logic as a foundational paradigm for machine intelligence. That... would make it quite easy, wouldn't it? Instead, we have a statistical paradigm that requires enormous complexity and huge amounts of compute. The basic paradigm of AI that dependend on logic - *symbolic AI* - famously failed to scale.

We can't analyse these statistical, data-driven models... or can we?

## The beast
Neural networks comprise of statistical pattern matches that have remarkable ability to encode non-linear relations between inputs and outputs. Their powers are not encoded: they emerge from interactions between training data and the weights that define them. These emergent qualities range from highly simplistic (classify the colour of one's eyes) to complex (figure out if the person who has these eyes is a biologically male or female despite being trained to denote the heart disease risk in the person) [1]. 

People don't really know how these emergent properties work. As a statistical physicist who has worked with emergent phenomena, I can tell you now that emergence is *genuinely* incredibly hard to characterise and detect. Emergence is fundamentally a concern of complex systems: the slogan of the field is that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. For relatively simple systems, you can trace the basic mechanisms that give way emergent capabilities: as noted in [1], researchers can isolate circuits within neural networks that, say, correspond the detection mechanisms for a particular category.

I am *immediately* skeptical about how this recognition capability scales to larger systems. Transformers are massive, complex and contain highly nonlinear interactions like vector concatenation. How do you interpret *that*? I can maybe see how you can carry out a process like this with an image, but if your input is natural langugage or dynamics. There's also the issue of *polysemanticity*, where multiple concepts are shoved into a single neuron. 

One the immediate concepts that I brushed over just now is the idea of a circuit in general: a subgraph within the full neural network that implements a human-hunderstandable implementation. There are thus a number of nested goals here:
1. Identifying the purpose of individual neurons within a model,
2. Studying the *features* that a single layer of the network appears to correspond to.
3. Identifying the circuits that these neurons and features contribute to.

Coming up with methods to achieve these goals is what we might call *mechanistic intepretability*. This is, in principle, the reverse engineering of neural networks from their weights into human readable algorithms. 

## Okay, we understand a neural network. So what? 
I suppose the first reason why one might be interested in mechanistic interpretability is that it's **cool as fuck**. However, one might also be interested because of the immediate safety considerations that the ultimate form of the field might enable. 

Say you carry out some really cool technique and you now have the capability to understand a neural network inside and out. You now can now [2]
* assess the rationale behind decisions that the network takes,
* identify and avoid the failure modes that are hidden within the model,
* actually demonstrate those failure modes conclusively in the first place,
* design reinforcement learning algorithms that negatively reinforce the activations that result in that behaviour,
* build guard rails that kick in when those activations are detected in the first place.

Why isn't mechanistic interpretability everywhere, then? The issue is that mechanistic interpretability is *hard*. Neural networks are by definition compression machines: they take enormous amounts of training data and compress it into a representation that allows for dense contextual association. This process of compression might actually be the main driver of the phenomenon of polysematicity, where the same activations are capable of being utilized to express completely different conceptual streams within the network itself. This emergence of polysemanticity might spell doom for dream of genuinely uncovering the way a neural network thinks... 

The BlueDot Impact blog mentions a program of removing polysemanticity, although it might require the sacrifice of efficiency and performance, where dense information coding is sacrificed for explainability. There are myriad techniques that can be employed for the purpose of carrying out the necessary untangling: some that I can find are
* simply making your neural network bigger to assign separate directions to different features,
* use a particular kind of model called a sparce autoencoder to decompose polysemantic neurons into monosemantic neurons,
* apply training objectives to penalize dense activations in the first place. This could be done on the fine-tuning level too, right? Use RL to minimise some metric of polysemanticity. I wonder how this could be specified and scaled...?
* Use probing classifiers that train on individual layers. The general approach here is to use a small model that is trained on the internal activations of a larger pre-trained network to discover what information is actually stored inside specific layers. The idea here is that if a probe achieves high accuracy, it proves that the targetted information is explicitly encoded and easily readable in that layer... but this seems odd to me. If you have a complex probe, you might end up just memorizing the task itself! What if the information necessary to classify is convincingly spread out through the full network: that is, if you use a probing classifier on all the layers and end up just getting a high accuracy on the entire system? Not so keen on this approach.
* Another important technique is chain-of-thought monitorability, where you essentially scan the intermediate thoughts of a reasoning model (that is effectively trained to explain it's thought process). This is an important branch of techniques, but the basic issues of scheming still emerge here: there just isn't much in the way of RL pressure to incentivize agents for getting the right answer. Models are also well capable of obfuscating their thoughts and oftentimes actually learn to come up with their *own* slang when operating in swarms! [6]
* Speaking of swarms, another useful approach is the construction of *model organisms* that propose the deliberate engineering of smaller, highly capable models that exhibit specific subcomponents of larger dangerous models. This is an essential technique in biology and could serve as a lab for studying mechanistic interpretability techniques.

### An aside: complex systems and an analogy to thermodynamics
A neural network is essentially a strongly coupled high dimensional system that, as mentioned before, displays chaoatic and emergent behaviour. Their training processes, where billions of parameters are tuned in order to capture associations between data points, represent the traversal of an exceptionally rugged loss landscape. 

There is a similar system in physics that demonstrates some of the thorniest theoretical problems within the field: glassy systems. The analogy between the glassy system and of neural networks emerges when you consider the correspondence between the loss function and the energy landscape that shows up in statistical physics. The energy landscape of a glass is rough and chaotic, just like the loss surface that a neural network exhibits. The incredibly difficult process of optimizing towards the ground state of a glass (and the subsequent failure to achieve this optimium) is thought to be the reason that the glass state essentially locks into a solid state, even whilst demonstrating the structureless composition of a liquid. Obtaining a theory for how this works on the microscale is currently an unsolved problem in theoretical physics.

Mechanistic intepretability attempts to carry out the same kind of investigation. It takes the microscopic structure of a neural network - the individual neurons of a neural network - and attempts to study how those activations combine in features and circuits to provide a description of how those activations contribute to the macroscopic output of those weights in tandem. 

A statistical physicist who hasn't thought about glass yet might wonder: isn't that fine, though? We have amazingly rich and powerful theories that connect microscale phenomena to macroscale phenomena. Surely we can just map between and solve mechanistic intepretability once and for all?

Oh, you arrogant physicist! The basic ideas that power statistical thermodynamics break down when you apply them to glass. Glass violates the fundamental assumption of *ergodicity*, which means that you cannot just spend a lot of time optimizing to explore all the microstates in order to understand the macrostate. 

In neural networks, we have a similar situation: the loss landscape when optimizing is extraordinarily complex exploring it properly is genuinely quite difficult. You can't just run an optimizer and find all the minima, because there are an infinite number of minima. Indeed, you don't even *want* to find the global minima most of the time because that's probably an indication that you've overfitted. Of course, you might aim for the minima that has the lowest average loss for some universal dataset, but we're going into way too much theory here.

Another *unbelievably* complex feature about the glass landscape is that the glass state is history dependent. The physical properties of a glass change depending on the history of the equilibriation so far, which breaks another basic idea in statistical physics called the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. This is, roughly speaking, the idea that (in ML language) the steps taken by your Adam optimizer result in the overall convergence of your model weights to a standard minima. Anybody who has trained a complex neural network knows that your optimizer can take you into a wide variety of steps, meaning that the history of your optimization process is essential to understanding why and where your model has ended up. This is, coming back full circle, exactly the reason why it's so damned difficult to use standard statistical physics to understand the glass state! To wrap this up into a single term, glasses and neural networks are both *non-equilibrium systems*. They are processes that are, at any given point, frozen snapshots of a wider optimization process. Statistical physics has a bit to say about these, but much less than it has to say about equilibrium systems.

There are plenty of analogies, but I have to move on. The point I'm trying to make is that the theoretical ideas that underpin the dream of mechanistic interpretability are the same that physicists have been grappling with for many decades now. Does this mean that mechanistic interpretability is doomed? **Absolutely not**. It just means that the theoretical ideas required will require a *lot* of innovation.

There is a friction, however, between the two approaches of mechanistic interpretability and statistical physics. The former, from what I can see, explicity rejects averaging and attempts to uncover mechanisms through analysis. I will probably think about in depth sooner rather than later.

## Dissent in the ranks
Some people do not believe that mechanistic interpretability will lead to anything useful. These folks take my own reservations regarding mechanistic interpretability as another attempt at trying to reason with a complex adaptive system and extend it to the point that doing so just isn't possible. The field itself has gone through a few shifts where Neel Nanda, the main face of the field himself, has pretty much abandoned the idea of having a complete paradigm for reverse engineering a neural network [4]. However, I do want to point out that this doesn't damn the field entirely: I think that the sociological concerns inform more of the pessimism than technical limitations. Take glasses, again: physicists have been working on glass theories for a while. Seventy years seems about a reasonable estimate of how long serious work has been going on in this field. However, we do not have seventy years to spend on figuring out the complexities of neural networks, even if they are simpler than glasses. 

There are some wider criticisms that make the actual problem of mechanistic interpretability way, way harder than I might have described so far outside of just the polysemanticity issue:
1. The approach itself is fundamentally reductionistic, which is a massive criticism of most approaches to working with complex systems in the first place [5].
2. AI architectures evolve quickly. The field made quite a lot of progress when all one had to think about was a convolutional neural network. A *reasoning model* is far more complex, and world models are proving to be even worse to characterise. How does a field that attempts to map out an architecture cope with the fact that the models themselves are changing?

## My final thoughts
Mechanistic interpretability is the elucidation of the connection between individual neurons, circuits and features to the behaviours of alignment and capability. 

There are strong parallels with mechanistic interpretability and the scientific method in general, which is to be expected given that reverse engineering is essentially the same thing. It is the process of studying the parts in order to make sense of the whole, which in turn implies the possibility of using that understanding to change how the whole itself works. As a former physicist, I can see why so many people from this field and the wider STEM ecosystem believe this to be a powerful and useful paradigm for getting to the core of AI safety.

I wonder however whether the basic flaws of the **reductionist** approach in general will manifest in this field, more so than they've manifested already. The entire discipline of complex systems was founded as a counter to the idea that you can take a vast and complex system and understand how it works by analysing its components. You cannot use a reductionist approach to studying the human brain: this much as always been clear. Why expect the approach to work on neural networks? I suppose you can make the bet that neural networks are, fundamentally, nothing more than mathematical objects. They are amenable to investigation in a way that most complex systems like the human brains are not, as their basic structure is not hidden. Weights are just numbers, and numbers can be analysed.

The field itself is representative of a grand quest that has emerged in many fields, but it is far too early to tell whether this will be the cornerstone of the technical side of alignment. 

I'm not quite sure whether this is going to be what I focus on in my own alignment work. It is wildly interesting, but I'm quite a bit more interested in the side of the field that *engineers* alignment: mechanistic interpretability is based on understanding systems and exploiting that understanding would surely be incredibly useful in alignment, but the basic act of understanding is not alignment unto itself. This is an approach that could support alignment: the techniques that actually bring about alignment will likely be found in another discipline.
