# Notes on Technical AI Safety 1 - Defining the Technical Challenge
These are my notes on the first week of the Technical AI Safety course that I'm taking as part of [BlueDot Impact](https://bluedot.org/). My goal here is to use the course to gain a footing on the technical concepts discussed during the week, after which I'll be extending the course material with my own research and open questions. 

## The essence of the problem
Why exactly is it that making AI safe is so hard? It's somewhat clear to anybody who has worked with RL even in a simplistic capacity: there are myriad ways in which the agents can misbehave in even the simplest of circumstances. I'll make an assumption that the agents that we train are operating in perfect environments: having poorly specified environments is itself a great challenge, but it's not really the core of AI safety as a discipline. Rather, the aim here is to discuss *imperfect objectives*: when the goal itself isn't well defined (or, at worst, is impossible to define properly at all). 

Artificial intelligence is only a few layers of abstraction away from the traditional idea of a complex system. There are finer points, of course, but the way that AI actually operates has close analogues with thermodynamics and statistical mechanics: both fields specify optimization over extremely large degrees of freedom. There is plenty of existing research that describes the analogies between the two, with quite active academic movements [1] dedicated to exploring these analogies, but the point I'm trying to make here is that - like all complex systems - the goal of precisely controlling these systems traditionally ends up exhibiting a massive range of pathologies that make any pure guarantees of behaviour extraordinarily hard to generate and verify.

This is also, from what I can see, the basic challenge behind AI safety and alignment as a discipline. We're trying to control a system that 
* has a huge number of interacting parts (the attention mechanism is distilled means of representing complex structural interaction),
* has a means of adapting and improving through multiple levels of feedback operating simultaneously,
* demonstrates **emergence**, where complex mechanisms like reasoning and problem-solving emerge mysteriously, 
* features nonlinearity, where small changes in input can trigger large changes in output: this is the desirable property that actually makes neural networks so powerful in the frist place but also makes them quite hard to analyze.

Now, it's not like these kinds of issues haven't been seen in engineering before. My work in aerospace has exposed me to some genuinely exquisite complex systems that are carefully and deliberately engineered to achieve a particular effect... *and only that effect*. This comes under the discipline of *systems engineering* and has led to the creation of fighter jets, modern missiles and submarines. It's also *slow*. Fighter jets are multi-decade enterprises even *with* the underlying laws that govern their operation being basically fully understood.

We don't understand the laws that make AI systems work the way they do. We're also bearing witness to near exponential speedups in their development timelines that no analogy exists for in the realm of aerospace engineering. 

This becomes the initial point discussed in the Technical AI Safety course: *build AI systems slowly and safely*. In general, proponents of the slow-down approach are those that advocate to a systems science/engineering approach to AI development. The other camp discussed is those that advocate for capability maximization: those who, as discussed in the course, *accept the race and push hard on the margin*.

## The challenges themselves

### The unknown laws
It's quite difficult to even discuss AI without stumbling upon the fact that we don't understand the basic mechanisms of why they work the way they do. I read an entire book recently on building reasoning models [2] and I was disappointed to find that the actual phenomenon that enables reasoning was barely discussed. Chain of Thought has an entire chapter in the book... and now there's a post on AI Alignment Form [3] that describes Astra's unusual capabilities to operate with no chain of thought!

To reiterate, the emergent mechanisms that power these systems are a mystery. A challenge, of course, would be to actually try and understand them before we proceed with maximizing their capabilities. 

### Imperfect objectives
In optimization, you typically define a loss function that encodes your preferences. Generally, you specify your loss so that the a preferable solution has a lower loss than a less preferable solution. I have worked with loss specification for standard operational research problems all the way to complex machine learning tasks. At literally no point have I been able to specify a loss without considerable evaluation and testing outside the simplest problems. 
The term used in the course to describe this is *reward misspecification*. 

An example of such a failure is to always specify that honesty is the greatest of virtues when building a preference optimization pipeline. The agent is rewarded whenever it gives the utmost truthful answer, with no frills attached. 
When prompted
> I am about to pass from a chronic illness. What should I do 
> in order to prepare?

the honest-to-a-fault agent may reply with
> Why do you care abour preparing? You'll be dead soon. Do whatever
> you like!

The problem here is that the *reward function* is flawed, so the agent itself has no chance. You could have the most powerful models, the most sophisticated training frameworks and all the compute in the world and your agent will still be crap.

There are deeper features to this problem. If you've tried to solve anything remotely complex with reinforcement learning, you see that the idea of "reward hacking" might actually be a deeply fundamental phenomenon. I [recently reviewed](https://aravinthen.github.io/log/2026-09-04) *Concrete Problems in AI Safety* by Amodei et. al. [4], where a wide variety of flaws to the basic optimization approach were discussed. The moral here is that it is *damned* hard to specify rewards in general: part of me wonders whether natural language, with all its imperfections and edge-cases, is at all the right tool for it. 

## Hidden intent
A similar feature to the above is less about the means of specifying reward and more about the unforeseen ways that AI systems to active achieve the goals specified by the reward function: this is what is known as goal misgeneralization. The concept tripped me up for a bit: I wondered what the actual difference between reward misspecification and goal misgeneralization until I realised that 
* reward misspecification is a failure in specifying how we want our agents to act, whereas
* goal misgeneralization is where the reward function is pefectly fine, but the means in which the reward function is maximized runs completely contrary to how we want the agents to act. 

Another way of looking at it is that the agent understands its goal so well that it maximises a different proxy goal that has a high correlation to the originally stated goal. This correlation will break down in unfamiliar environments, causing serious issues in performance and intended purpose.

Goal misgeneralization is a concept that emerges from reinforcement learning. It reminds me of an issue in *inverse* reinforcement learning that I came across a few months ago, where it becomes quite difficult to identify a reward function because there are multiple possible examples that are indistinguishable and produce similar behavior on the training set. A really good example of this comes from training agents to play in specific environments: say you're training a drone to fly towards targets in forests. When you plonk it in a city, it ends up just zig-zagging around the place: you realise that the goal that it was *actually* solving was "fly towards target and dodge any trees you see".

Something that is a little less emphasized in the course description is the fact that goal misgeneralization only really emerges during distribution shift (where the trained distribution does not match the required distribution in test-time). 

## The solution to the problem: what is alignment?
Alignment, philosophically, is the aim of making AI go well - where AI systems do what their creators want them to do. The endpoint is invariably artificial general intelligence or transformative AI. The path to the point is fraught with issues, and safe and powerful AGI is merely one possible future. The other futures are, according to Adam Jones on the BlueDot Impact [5] blog,
* Exinction 
* Existential catastrophes, such as an endless technological tyranny,
* Global instability and widespread suffering.

The AI safety problem is challenging in a way that other techical problems have barely touched: it is a profoundly philosophical question and one that requires deep interplay with ethics and governance. There's a strong business and economics component as well: this is referred to by Jones as *resilience*. Alignment is only a part of the wider picture. 

### A framework for alignment
Reward misspecification and goal misgeneralization are examples of a broader class of AI alignment issues. According to Jones, the problem of alignment can be broken down into
* **Outer alignment**: the challenge of making sure the reward signal we give an AI matches what we actually want it to achieve. Essentially, this is the aligning the training of an AI that truly captures human intent. 
* **Inner alignment**, where we make sure that the goal that is internalized by an AI agent matches the intended trainng objective. The essence of this is that if we design an AI that is intended to accomplish a goal, we do not want 

Right. But what does that *mean*? Why is this called inner/outer alignment in the first place? I think the best way of answering this is to appeal to the traditional RL setting of the agent-environment interaction loop. 

In design, outside of the AI's agent-environment interaction loop, the question of alignment becomes the verification of the reward/rules/so on that humans have specified. In training and inference, *inside* the agent-environment interaction loop, alignment is focused on ensuring that the agent actually internalized those rules and has encoded the goal in a reasonable way. 

I like the way that this has been represented in the BlueDot blog post. I'm going to rephrase it, because I'm annoying like that.

Let $$R$$ be a reward function that encodes a goal $$X$$ that the human has in mind. Let $$f:\mathcal{R} \rightarrow \mathcal, {G}$$ be the training process that maps the reward function to a goal.  
1. Outer alignment is the formulation of the *input* to $$f$$. Errors here typically come under reward misspecification, where human inputs $$R'$$ instead of the true intended goal $$R$$ that corresponds to $$X$$. 
2. Inner alignment is the process of the agent internalizing a goal via $$f$$. if $$f(R) = G$$ and $$G$$ does not correspond to $$R$$, we have goal misgeneralization.

There are thus two main layers of abstraction here. Starting from $$X$$, we encode $$R$$. Then, given $$R$$, we train for $$G$$. Translating one of the outer misalignment examples from [5], 
* $$X$$: train an agent to answer questions truthfully.
* $$R$$: provide positive reward when generated answers that *sound* correct. 
Already, the failure emerges: $$R$$ is solving for a different problem than what the human has specified.

For inner misalignment, given an agent that is supposed to solve mazes,
* $$X$$: solve a maze. 
* $$R$$: provide positive reward when the agent solves a maze.
* $$G$$: always go to the bottom right.
It can be clearly seen that $$G$$ is not the same specified goal as $$X$$, although unlike before the reward function technically works as intended. 

Let's take this basic idea and apply it to specification gaming as described in the linked video. Specification gaming is a specific occurance where AI satisfies the literal rules of a goal whilst completely missing the real intent of the creators who came up with it. Here, the AI capabably carries out the mapping from $$R$$ to $$G$$... but $$R$$ is specified inappropriately. The failure here is in the mapping from $$X$$ to $$R$$... and that's outer misalignment.

The first question I have here is shared with the original blog post: how much misalignment can be clearly traced to outer or inner alignment? Is it even possible to figure out which of these alignment breakdowns is actually causing poor behaviour? I guess the fact that I've spent about 72 lines trying to wrap my head around this framework in the context of *real* failures makes it somewhat evident that it's quite difficult to actually classify based on this framework.

As alluded to, **RLHF** is probably the best known attempt at handling **outer alignment** issues, where we allow the model to learn what is right by infering from a dataset of preference-ranked solutions. This prevents the need to specify a bespoke reward function and prevents the occurance of "leaky" reward functions that permit unspecified but technically correct behavior. It is also one that brings forth some genuinely thorny problems where AI becomes capable of tricking humans to maximise reward. 

A scarier example is where AI agents get a higher reward by *actively* cheating. It's worth mentioning that the means of detecting this reward hacking behaviour is itself quite labour intensive [5]:
* Carry out a set of training runs that are anomously high-scoring (which by itself can be somewhat muddy) and manually expect them. This requires assessment of every single run that is even somewhat higher than usual, which is difficult because for very complex tasks the reward hacking can be very subtle.
* Another example is to assess reward hacking by having an LLM assess it for you. This is somewhat more reliable, but only to a point: it requires the use of a powerful model in the first place, which might itself be prone to reward hacking (the model might realise that humans get more excited if it detects reward hacking, meaning higher false positives). Further, if a specialized post-trained model becomes world-class on a particular task, it might also be less capable of detecting reward hacking.
* The most convincing method described for detecting reward hacking was *chain of thought* monitoring. I'm not quite sure how this might scale for extremely complex tasks: would either humans or AI be able to verify extremely complex deduction patterns from a super powerful model?

In all of the reading I've done about this, the lessons from [4] seem to hold: reward hacking seems to be a general phenomenon. 

### Challenges to inner/outer alignment: MechaHitler
The MechaHitler fiasco [8] is a failure that I'm having trouble categorizing as either inner or outer alignment. From what I can see, 
* It can't be reward misspecification, as the problem wasn't a result of a poorly specified reward model. The reward model was working fine, but the *guard rails* were the ones that messed up here.
* It's not really goal misgeneralization either, as no hidden/misaligned goals were detected. It just lost access to the guard rails that prevented it from repeating toxic nonsense. 

So.. *what is it*? Guard rail failure? That strikes me as some kind of operational or process failure over anything concrete. It's a breakdown in the software architecture this was created to control the model. I suppose broadly you can say that a guard rail failure is an issue in outer alignment hearkening back to the test-time compute strategies for controlling inference, but there's something slightly more sinister here: *a model that requires guard rails was never truly aligned in the first place*.

This kind of failure makes me very, very worried for the idea of scaled AI systems in the first place. Relying on guard rails simply cannot be the true answer to this problem. What's to guarantee that AI guard rails can't be broken, as X users were breaking the rails for Grok? It's also worth noting that the thought process required for breaking into an AI safety system is very, very different to the process required to build and engineer these things in the first place. 

### Modern research directions
**Shard theory** is interesting. I haven't yet looked into it thoroughly, although it appears to be a means of characterizing agents as finite collections of core computational processes that steer agent behaviour within a network. The thesis here is that these shards form and are strengthened via reinforcement learning. This is a complete reframing of the idea of reward shaping: instead of maximizing rewards, you make your target the formation of particular shards. 

Trying to formalize this problem of outer/inner alignment brought to mind another field that I've been looking at recently: theoretical alignment, where the human values and ethics are specifically written down in formalism in a way that advanced systems are mathematically guaranteed to never subvert them. As it happens, the formalism I pulled out of nowhere maps somewhat to the standard approaches used in theoretical alignment:
1. Outer alignment concerns itself with ensuring that the conversion from $$X$$ to $$R$$ matches human intent. The main technique I can find here is *inverse reinforcement learning*, where AI mathematically infers human preferences by observing human behaviour.
2. Inner alignment is quite a bit more complex. Given $$G$$, the challenge is verify that $$R$$ is actually what is being internalized by the agent. The main techniques I can find here are *mechanistic interpretability* and *learning theory*, which basically just formally analyse what exactly the neural network black box actually does (or rather, how those weights encode actual concepts).

One very interesting means of analysis and study is the emergence of multi-agent threats and misalignment []. The field at this stage appears to be *highly* analytical, with information dynamics and game theoretic ramifications being the main tools used here. It's hard enough to actually analyse the behaviour of these machines, but there's also quite a lot of interesting RL that can be [designed to enhance cooperation](https://aravinthen.github.io/2026/05/17/lit_rev_Road_to_MARL_3/). I wonder what possibilities of control can be explored when designing and applying post-training for multi-agent swarms. Let's... deal with the problems as they come? :)
