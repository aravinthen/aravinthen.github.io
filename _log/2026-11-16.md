## Game theory
1. Recall: a strategic game, or a normal form game, is a scenario where every player has a set of possible actions and preference ordering over the set of possible action profiles. It's where the player chooses her action once and for all, with these choices being made simultaneously.
2. Ah, the crux is here! Labelling the Cartesian product of all of the outcomes as $$A$$, the feature of the problem that distinguishes the strategic game from a decision problem is that the value of the player's action is intrinsically linked to the actions taken by other players. That is a very nice modelling distinction, and it wraps a lot of complexity in a single descriptor!
3. What are the preference relations that emerge when thinking about alignment? Humans prefer a safe and powerful AI. The training process of an AI prefers a powerful AI, as safety is harder to ensure and focusing on safety results in less powerful AI. 
4. Nash equilibrium: a situation where no player can profitably deviate. It's a *steady state* of a game, a point where iterating over strategies based on preference will eventually lead you.
5. The basic way of finding Nash equilibria is to find the best response function for a player: that is, a mapping between all actions of the opponent to the subsequent best actions of the player. Then, the Nash equilibria is the profile of actions for which *every* player is operating with their best response.
6. Ooh, the Mozart and Mahler game is quite a nice illustration of what the poorly named Battle of the Sexes is trying to communicate, which is *coordination despite conflicting interests*.
