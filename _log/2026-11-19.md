## Summary
1. Read *Debates and Decisions: On a Rational of Argumentation Rules* by Glazer and Rubinstein.

## BlueDot Technical AI Safety Project
1. I was wondering yesterday what the *essence of debate* was. It's a mechanism, but for what? This is the paper that I've found that sort of answers that query.
2. Debate is described as *a mechanism by which an uninformed decision maker extracts information from two informed debaters*. It is a process of 
    * Extracting information,
    * Evaluating it,
    * Synthesizing it into a decision output.
3. This is my first explicit *mechanism design* paper. The mechanism here is the debate rules and the intended behavior is the maximised probability that the listener reaches the right conclusion. 
4. Ah, but there are constraints - length and form. There's also a very interesting game theoretic result: under optimal debate rules, the conclusion drawn by the judge is not necessarily the same conclusion that he would have drawn if he had literally interpreted the information during the debate. I suppose in a sense this acts as an *averaging* of arguments - something that AI agents are really good at!
5. The optimal design rules for debate must be built such that they manage the dependency on iteration between arguments and counterarguments. 
6. The purpose of debate is interesting. The authors of the paper describe argument for the sake of argument, but what if one can host a debate as a means of juxtaposition of different viewpoints? This challenges the notion that debates need by zero-sum games.
7. The authors address this be explicitly describing a debate where informed agents hold differing views. They also describe a situation where the judge of the debate might be unconvinced by either argument! The core here is that the strengths of the arguments depend on the capabilities of both agents to provide counter arguments.
8. **Debate consistency**: it is impossible that 'x wins the debate' if y is brought up as a counterargument to x, but 'y wins the debate' if x is brought up as a counterargument to y. This is an ordering statement: it's impossible for the result of a debate to be different based on whether x or y is presented first. I also think that this is a property of the judge: a judge that chooses x over y when x is the main point and y is the rebuttal cannot logically choose differently if x and y are switched.
9. ... however, this is *not* necessarily a property of debate rules if the goal is to extract information from the debaters!
10. Debates are modelled as combinations of 
    * procedural rules, which specify argument order and the kinds of arguments debaters are allowed to raise,
    * a persuasion rule, which specifies the relationship between the arguments presented in the debate and the outcome chosen by the listener.
11. This is *sort of* a simplified version of the debate game described yesterday. In the formulation of the debate in this paper, constraints are specified directly: they have an action space which includes the capability of raising arguments that support their preferred outcome, as well as raising counterarguments to points raised by the opponent. There are also levels of complexity to debate that need to be controlled: here, the authors have decided complexity based on the procedural rules of the debate. Further, after arguments are made, the summarised terminal states are called outputs O1 and O2. It is the job of the judge/listener to decide between these two. 
12. There are also measures that describe the outcome of an incorrect conclusion. In stochastic games where the output decision is probabilistic, there is no obviously no pure Nash equilibria.
13. Foundationally, the aim of this paper is make the idea of strict context necessary for optimality in a debate. I'm satisfied that this is the main output necessary for making sense of this paper as a contribution.
