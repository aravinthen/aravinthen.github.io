## Literature 
1. This is a very interesting paper, not just because it broadens my perspectives on how AI models can fail, but because it describes the non-reinforcement learning based failure points that I've become somewhat less accustomed to.
2. These guys talk about world models ten years before world models were considered a frontier objective. Damn. Prescient.
3. Not quite sure about this idea of "empowerment". An entropy measure for the enviromnent might be more appropriate. You can typically consider irreversibility in environments by defining an entropy measure that is maximised over time. Of course, physical entropy is pretty damned hard to characterize too.
4. "Reward hacking may be a deep and general problem, and one that we believe is likely to become more common as agents and environments increase in complexity." - Hm. Interesting. I wonder what this looks like from a geometric perspective? In fairness, reward hacking takes place in real life systems too - crack addicts, for one, are just gaming their dopamine systems. Maybe the solution in general isn't to *avoid* reward hacking but to guard against it geometrically? 
5. The problem of abstract rewards has largely been handled by LLMs. interesting indeed.
6. Goodhart's law: *when a metric is used as a target, it ceases to be a good metric.* I ought to remember that, especially when speaking to managers.
7. *Oooooh*. A really interesting idea here. Designing an explicit *reward* agent that actively seeks out reward hacking examples. That's really cool. How might such an agent be trained? I quite like the idea of adversarial blinding too, where the agent is explicitly prevented from actively learning it's reward function to the point of gaming it. I suppose the really odd thing here is that, in the process of training an agent, it builds a representation of the reward function over building a representation of the value function.
8. That would be interesting: using mechanistic interpretability techniques to figure out how an agent actually models the reward function. 
9. Careful engineering, haha. The most unrealistic advice in this paper.
10. A compound set of rewards being more difficult to hack seems... off. Doesn't that have a risk of providing more hackable targets?
11. **Scalable oversight**: the challenge of providing reliable supervision, evaluation and control over advanced AI systems. Oversight is when you provide detailed guidance to an agent and scalable oversight is the ability to provide such guidance as agents become more complex and harder to interpret.
12. One of the most important manifestations of this control is *reinforcement learning with human feedback*. The first approach to the so-called semi-supervised RL that is described here is reminiscient of RLHF... it's in interesting umbrella of techniques. Before thinking about RLHF, one ought to be considering the generic proble of scalable oversight.
13. It's quite interesting to see how hierarchical reinforcement learning is described as a means of scalable oversight. My first instinct would be that this would add multiple layers of complexity, but I can also see how breaking down the action space into easily understandable components could allow more appropriate analysis. 
14. It ought to be mentioned that most of these ideas fall under the umbrella of explainability. You can provide reliable supervision, evaluation and control when you *understand* why the system is doing what it is doing. 
15. **Safe exploration**: where an agent is given the ability to explore safely. Yeah, this is almost all derivative of RL theory.
16. Something that isn't so much described here is the way that the algorithms that train these machines can influence the way that they operate. The SARSA/Q-learning difference comes to mind here: could it be possible to encode safer problem approaches into the RL technique employed?
17. **Robustness to distributional change**: the essence of the general intelligence problem. Distributional shift occurs when an AI agent is put into situations where its training hasn't prepared it to operate. 
18. ...*but this is hard for humans too!* I suppose the problem is to figure out how to operate in a state of ignorance. I suppose the essence of this problem is to first figure out how to *detect* this ignornance in the first place. 
19. I also guess that LLMs have a reasonable strong ability to generalise to intellectual tasks given that they've been trained on enormous amounts of condensed human thought.
