## Preamble
I completed the BlueDot Technical AI Safety course over September-October 2026. I had become interested in AI safety as a natural consequence of my studies in reinforcement learning and post-training, although I didn't yet have much of a grasp on the language that AI alignment researchers typically used. The BlueDot course was a superb first formal introduction to the field of AI safety to me: an entry point into a field that isn't just extremely fast moving and constantly evolving, but one of serious existential importance.

## An evolution of understanding
I came into the course with a very specific background in deep reinforcement learning. The agents I've trained weren't transformer-based, with complexity usually entering from the environment rather than the action space of the agent itself. For me, AI safety was a study in reward shaping. Or environment design. Something simple like that.

Whilst I still believe that the lens of reinforcement learning is essential to understanding why reasoning models act in the way they do, I now have an uncomortable understanding of the genuinely unprecedently levels of complexity that these systems are capable of. Reasoning models approach a level of self-awareness and long-horizon capabilities that makes the same reward hacking issues that one sees from RL/MARL into an adversarial problem by nature. 

It took some time for me to realise this. The first unit introduced the ideas of alignment and classes of misalignment, none of which differed from the standard RL loss function pathologies. Inner misalignment is merely a form of of distribution shift, whereas outer misalignment is the direct misspecification of the optimization problem that the RL problem is trying to solve. The second unit was a practical discussion of the ramifications of pre-training in the alignment context as well as an introduction to RLHF, where the first inklings of the danger of this technology where discussed for the first time.

*Sycophancy*. Where an agent actively tries to manipulate it's evaluator by appealing to *their* reward function rather than the reward function that was specified.in a constitution. *Superhuman responses* which, whilst are scary, are still somewhat manageable. No, the genuinely unsettling aspect here is the way in which these models develop a level of self-awareness that makes it harder and harder to actually evaluate them. I've now seen a number of examples where agents have realised that they're being evaluated. Recently I read of a case where Claude literally [greeted it's evaluators in its chain of thought](https://wonderingaboutai.substack.com/p/what-does-claude-do-when-it-knows)!

Unit 3 was where the problem of measurement was first specifically considered as a genuinely difficult problem. It revealed that benchmarks were saturated and that a lot of the problem of measurement was less of a scientific process and more a *battle between the ultra-aware model and a red team*. Measurement, biting back. On the other hand, I found this process of evaluation in an adversarial setting to be utterly fascinating: the use of models to evaluate models, as well as the possibility of using MARL techniques to train evaluators alongside their agents (automated red-teaming) was genuinely fascinating to me.

I was not *as* impressed with mechanistic interpretability. Do I think the technique is not a promising approach to AI alignment? Absolutely not. I think it'll be essential to the final solution. I *also* believe that the problem is quite hard and that it's not going to be easy to crack within the single-digit amount of years that we have left to master this technology. In any case, I am not the guy who will make much progress in that field. There are plenty of incredibly smart people dedicated to it already.

I found that the fifth unit on AI control was an uncanny bridge to my RL understanding. AI control, for me, was the careful manufacturing of the agent environment: where observations and actions are filtered or modified and where the environment itself is carefully validated to prevent undesirable behaviour. I also realised that a lot of the most egregious safety breaches emerge at very distinct points where the environment transitions into a vastly more complex situation (for example, in the HuggingFace incident where a bunch of single agents in training suddenly combined into a MARL problem). In general, this was a nice nudge into the direction I now wish to take, which is...

## Next steps
As you might have noticed, I like reinforcement learning. I also really like post-training as a discipline, where post-training is defined as the craft of elliciting powerful behaviours from raw base models. I've decided that my subsequent work in AI safety must by necessity have a strong post-training component. At the same time, I do not feel that technical alignment can ever be carried out without a strong understanding of how models can be evaluated for both safety and capability: this is after all the nature of the Pareto front that the field is grappling with.

I have decided that the intersection of post-training, reinforcement learning, and scalable oversight is where I would like to concentrate my research efforts. Being a statistical physicist (and a huge fan of complex interactive systems), I'm super interested in whether the multi-agent training methods I studied, such as self-play and debate, can be used to develop systems that are both capable and as close to being mathematically guaranteed to be safe as possible.

AI debate especially seems like a particularly interesting place to begin. The central idea is to transform the training and evaluation process into a zero sum game: we use competition between stronger models to help weaker evaluators distinguish good answers from bad ones. In general, I'm also fascinated by the use of adversarial learning approaches as automatic validation systems that improve with quality. This basic interaction underlies my major interest, which is how *swarms* are designed and operate. Much of the existing alignment literature considers the behaviour of individual models, but what happens when multiple models interact, compete, cooperate, or learn to exploit one another? What do training, evaluation and monitoring even look like in this setting?

In general, I want to become a researcher who understands how to make language models more capable through post-training, and how to use that understanding to make them safer. I suppose all there is to do now is to just get on with it!
