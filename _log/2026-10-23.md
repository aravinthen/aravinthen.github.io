## Reinforcement learning
1. A brief history of reinforcement learning with human feedback:
    * The basic approach of reinforcement learning from preferences comes from the paper *TAMER: Training and Agent Manually via Evaluative Reinforcement*. 
    * An actor-critic algorithm (*COACH*) was developed soon after: this is just where human feedback is used to tune the advantage function. Presumably, this is where the *critic* is trained with explicit human input.
    * The primary reference where human feedback is used specifically for *alignment* is Christiano et. al 2017. It should be noted that *reward modelling* is the real advancement here, where preference data is employed to build a condensed model of reward.
    * Following this, there was an explosion of RLHF when companies began scaling LLMs. The basic reference here is *Fine-Tuning Language Models from Human Preferences* by Ziegler et al. (2019).
2. "RLHF, while optimizing a simple loss function, involves traning different AI models *in sequence* and linking them together in a complex online optimization.". That's the kicker here: all we are doing is optimization.
3. "For continuing tasks, we often take T to infinity and rely on discounting to keep the objective well defined" - I love how a section of Sutton and Barto is summarised in a sentence!
4. Most of chapter 3 is known to me - there are a ton of notes in other log posts. Still, it's nice to go into detail.
5. One thing is quite nice to keep in mind, although part of me wonders if it's somewhat reductive. The book describes the states of the domain to be prompts sampld from a training dataset, whereas the action is the completion of the prompt. This also means that RLHF rewards are not discounted and carried out for an entire sequence of generated tokens (although rewarding sequences of actions isn't a problem in DRL).
6. This brings to question: *how does PPO work in RLHF*? Does it use GAE, which implicitly balances long/short term rewards? I know that DPO/GRPO are more frequently used, but...
7. I think the interpretation I found in a previous paper, where prediction of a token is considered an action, makes more sense to me. Granted, it's something of a moot point: prediction of a sequence of tokens still constitutes an action! The only difference here is that Lambert specifically notes that the horizon of RLHF is a single step (T=1) versus multistep.
8. Ah, this is an interesting point: RL starts with a randomized model, whereas RLHF starts from a strong pretrained base model. A lot of the science of RLHF is based around making sure that this capable prior isn't ruined via RL optimization. This also demystifies the elicitation theory of post-training: if you don't want your model to change much, it makes sense to have a really strong base model in the first place. 
9. Again, something to clarify: *RLHF* always penalizes changes to the *base model*. Not the model after every RL step! However, Lambert discusses how a lot of research goes into how to maximize the effect of a given KL budget. Interesting. This whole process is called *regularization*, or the active control of the optimization process.
10. Some post-training recipes use RL only on mathematical/verifiable problems and use alternative techniques in other domains. I'm realising that post-training *genuinely* is a mixture of techniques used to ellicit very specific behaviours...
