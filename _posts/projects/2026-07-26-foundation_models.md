I have spent six months dedicated to classical and deep reinforcement learning, with another month exploring trends in autonomous systems in modern artificial intelligence research. I'm now at a point where I have
* internalised the agent–environment interaction and the way reinforcement-learning algorithms translate from mathematical definitions into working software,
* implemented and evaluated methods spanning dynamic programming, Monte Carlo learning, temporal-difference learning, policy gradients, actor–critic methods and proximal policy optimisation,
* developed a stronger understanding of the experimental methodology required to evaluate learning systems reliably,
* begun to understand why modern autonomous systems increasingly combine reinforcement learning with large pretrained models, learned representations, external tools and scalable evaluation systems.

Throughout this time, I've been very conscious of the fact that I'm not operating on the frontier of autonomous systems research. In a sense, it feels as though I've just finished an apprenticeship in the classical route to the field without ever have even touched the subject matter that will actually play a part in how autonomous systems will actually be implemented. 

I think I'm now ready to move my research interests closer to frontier AI research. I had two options: 
1. World models, which are specialized and compressed representations that are capable of accurately predicting a range of dynamical systems (including reality itself)
2. Foundation models, which are sequence models trained on very general subject matter that can then be guided to solve specific tasks with superhuman accuracy. 

I've decided that, due to the resources available, I'll be tackling the latter. Both directions are relevant to my interests, but I've chosen to focus first on foundation models because they provide the most direct route into post-training, reasoning, language-agent research and the current research-engineering problems that interest me.
