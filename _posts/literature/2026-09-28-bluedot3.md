# Notes on Technical AI Safety 3 - The Science of Evals
My notes for Week 3 of the BlueDot Impact Technical AI Safety course. This unit is about evaluations - actually being able to assess whether or not an AI is safe or not. This is a complex, deeply experimental process. Having carried out post-training myself, I've seen how the process of evaluating an SFT run for positive signal can be a process of sorting out noise from signal. When considering safety, strong evaluation frameworks are paramount. 

## The two axes of evaluation
Assessing the risk of deploying an AI model comes down to two factors:
1. The ability to carry detect harmful actions in the first place, as well as characterizing what those actions actually are
2. Finding the likelihood of the model carrying out those harmful actions.
Unsafe models might, when probed, carry out harmful actions 100% of the time. Models with some level of guardrails might carry out those actions 3% of the time... and might carry out another class of dangerous action 60% of the time.

It looks as though trying to evaluate AI safety is like trying to fight a hydra. This, I daresay, is the essence of firefighting a truly complex system.

Evaluations are a multi-stage process, with proper evaluations taking place at every stage of the pretraining to post-training pipeline as well as pre-and-post deployment.

## Towards a science of evals
It seems as though the basic evaluation pipeline is relatively similar:
1. Get a model,
2. Train it, 
3. Test it on a set of benchmarks a couple of times and statistically assess its performance.

However, this approach falls apart in a few ways. Firstly, *LLMS are incredibly sensitive to input*. According to [1], subtle changes in prompt formatting can have enormous effects on the accuracy of the output. We are after all considering the generation of sequences of natural language: naturally, we will encounter dramatic levels of noise within input and ouput.

Here is a basic list of some interesting questions in the Evals space:
1. How do different phrasings of the same question can lead to completely different results?
2. Likewise, how do different evaluation structures lead to different results?
3. What metrics are required to accurately categorize model safety? 

Evals are not primarily a safety tool. They're mostly a *capabilities* evaluation tool, which is fine when you're trying to solve problems or evaluate the upper bound. There is a single very dangerous scenario where higher capability makes safety *much* harder to quantify alignment and safety, and that is where *the AI becomes so situationally aware that it is aware that it is being tested*. 

I want to say that whilst this is terrifying, *how marvellous*. How wonderful that this technology can even do something like this! Evaluation awareness [2] is a remarkable example of how we're working with a technology that challenges us in ways we aren't even equipped for. It's incredible that some models avoid blackmail *specifically* because they understand that assessments of blackmail is a test [3]! 

When studying resources for this, I am increasingly realising just how many pieces have been written that call attention to the problem but don't really provide much in the way of grounded technical solutions. This is a *nascent field*: people are genuinely struggling with figuring out ways to carry out this level of testing.

A useful resource that I've seen is this guide by *Apollo Research* [4], which goes through the full operation spectrum of carrying out evaluations. This is a really nice introductory resource, with clarity present from the very beginning: if you define evals as "the systematic measurement of properties in AI systems, then it stands to reason that any actual technical work involving LLMs comes down to understanding evals. 

## On Scheming 
Scheming is a hypothetic scenario where AI realized that it's being evaluated and is capable of faking safety and compliance. It's a very subtle and dangerous state, mostly because it's a case of the LLM *actively* trying to decieve the person who attempting to align it. In fact, an AI is faking being safe, making this an example of inner misalignment. 

I'm interested in scheming because there's a connect here to post-training. RLHF is desinged to reward safe behaviour and punish unsafe behaviour. However, if a model is capable of scheming, then the basic integrity of the RLHF pipeline falls apart: a model will be rewarded *not for actually being aligned, but for faking alignment*...

### Dealing with scheming
During research, I came across a few ways that teams work to both detect and control scheming behaviour. The coolest approach that I've come across is the engineering of *counterfactual* environments [5], where the model is tricked into believing that it's already been deployed. If a model thinks the safety guards are off, then it may suddenly reveal it's unaligned behaviour. 

Another approach is the careful auditing of CoT trajectories, where the traces are scanned for signs of situational awareness. This is an approach that I've found from the DeepMind Safety Research blog [6], but is also seemingly deployed basically everywhere else too.

Hold on. These are adaptive systems. Is there a shadow of a possibility that scheming training just makes your agent better at avoiding being caught scheming? In fact, there's no real way of knowing whether the agent actually wants to scheme or just knows that being caught scheming will cause it fail in the eyes of human evaluators...

### Game theory, my old friend
What, precisely, is it that makes the idea of scheming so disconcerting? 
I've spent a few days thinking about this and came to the realisation that it's the same aspect that makes any kind of adversarial scenarios difficult work with. The adversarial loop goes like this:
1. You come up with a strategy against your opponent.
2. They learn what you do and come up with the perfect counterstrategy.
3. You can now learn what they do and come up with the counterstrategy to their counterstrategy. 
4. ... where do you end up?
There are a few places where you might end up, but the common situation is that of a **Nash equilibrium** where you and your opponent have settled into a fixed point in your strategy cycle. 

Why am I talking about this? What does this have to do with Evals? If you recognize that measurement itself is an optimization problem whereby you're trying to minimize the uncertainty in your measurement, you come up with an analogous situation to the above case:
1. You come up with a benchmark for your agent. 
2. It trains and then it beats the benchmark. 
3. You come up with a new benchmark for your agent.
4. It trains and then becomes aware that it is being tested and generally develops its capability for situational awareness. 
5. You put the agent into a counterfactual environment.
6. It trains and then becomes capable of figuring out when it is in a counterfactual environment...

As you can see, you're literally just coming up with counterstrategy after counterstrategy as the agent you're evaluating does the same. You're measuring a system that becomes increasingly resistant to being measured! The only difference is that the process of "coming up with a new strategy" is automated with the agent.

This game-theoretic optimization pattern is prevalent in reinforcement learning and has powered some of the biggest breakthroughs in the field. It's sometimes called *self-play*, but self-play is a particular example of the technique. What we're really looking at here is the problem of learning dynamics, as written about [here](https://aravinthen.github.io/2026/04/26/lit_rev_Road_to_MARL_2/). 

This kind of game-theoretic thinking makes one wonder: perhaps our approach to evaluation is simply too one-sided. Measurement isn't the question here and neither is optimization (although the latter is a component of the solution).

The only advantage here, then, is that *the artificial intelligence system has a better best-response generator than we do*. Does this mean that the natural way of addressing the issue of scalable oversight is to *actively train* the evaluator, too? 

I thought I was really smart when I realised this, but cursory research reveals that OpenAI has carried out an [automated self-play based red-teaming approach](https://openai.com/index/unlocking-self-improvement-gpt-red/). Alas, I've been beaten to the punch... but there are some genuine pathologies that can emerge if you carry out self-play: the Nash equilibrium you discover could very well be one that isn't the one you want. That, however, is a topic for another time.

## How do companies actually do this stuff?
As part of the BlueDot Impact Technical AI Safety program, I'm supposed to come up with an overview of how actual AI labs ensure the safety of their models. Given that I've spent quite a bit of time already getting too excited about the automated red-team approach of OpenAI, let's go ahead and carry out a serious analysis of what OpenAI does to ensure safety.

### On limits
So, there are three basic catastrophic risk categories that OpenAI carefully tracks. The first two are quite general, whereas the last seems fundamentally like an AI-intrinsic risk factor. These categories are
1. Biological/chemical risks, 
2. Cybersecurity, which is described as the ability of an AI model to assist in cyberdefense and cyberoffense,
3. AI self-improvement, which is by far the most enigmatic of the risks that are described here. 

These three categories have a set of thresholds, of which High and Critical are the most interesting. From the document linked, it looks like "AI self-improvement" isn't yet assigned a threshold just yet. 

There are also categories that require ongoing research and careful assessment of the existing literature: research in 
* Long-range autonomy, or the capbaility of a model to execute a long-horizon sequence of actions sufficient for cybersecurity without being detected,
* Sandbagging, which is roughly speaking the ability to lie on evaluations,
* Autonomous replication and adaption, which is a shorthand for persistence despite design,
* Safeguard undermining,
* The capability to create/facilitate nuclear and radiological threats, completing the NR of the CB specified above.

Here, limits are applied whenever any of these categories cross into the critical threshold. The minute these capabilities cross this point, development is expected to cease.

### Protections
OpenAI maps protective measures to contain the catastrophic risks provided above. Many of these are standard and were discussed last week, but to recap I can find
* Security threat modelling and risk management, each which appear to be based around building a model of what can go wrong and carrying out continuous monitoring and validation.
* Defense in depth: this is a full *architecture* that comes into action to provide a layered security strategy. You have the safeguards that were mentioned last week: presumably this is the architectural implementation that maximises their effectiveness. i
* Of course, there are safeguards. However, the safeguards emerge from malicious users as well as misaligned models, which I found interesting: is there any other software technology where the product is also capable of carrying out harmful actions by nature?

This sounds very, very similar to a lot of what my girlfriend does for work (operational resilience in the financial industry). I wonder whether the discipline of operational resilience can inform AI safety efforts too?

### Evaluation
This is essentially what I've been studying all week. The basic set of techniques that are used here is three-fold:
1. Scalable evaluations, which measure and check whether any capability thresholds have been crossed. It looks like these are trained classifier models that continually assess outputs.
2. Red-teaming, as mentioned above.
3. Auditing, where a capabilities report is filed before external deployment. The interesting point here is that OpenAI has a group of leaders known as the safety advisory group that assess the capabilities report and approve/reject subsequent safety recommendations. 

### Response
Whenever a threshold is reached or forecasted to be reached, the developer commits to pausing. The purpose of this pause is to specify safeguards and relevant security controls that satisfy the threshold standards. It is, then, something of an iterative process of development.

### Accountability
The accountability that OpenAI carries out is also quite full featured. As mentioned, there's an internal group of leaders (SAG) that is responsible for the continuous review of capabilities and safeguards. The Preparedness Framework is also a living document and is continually reviewed, updated and made publically available.
Another feature that emerges here that could give way to the role of external AI safety organisations and auditors is are the provisions to work with third-parties for independent evaluations of model capabilities. 

## Personal opinion
It is very, very clear to me that the work and effort that goes into the role of AI preparedness necessitates not just a specialist but *an associated set of professionals*. I don't think that this is the kind of problem that can be solved by a bunch of technologists sitting in a lab: it is too interdisciplinary and requires genuine human oversight throughout the process. I anticipate that there will be AI safety auditors operating in the near future.
