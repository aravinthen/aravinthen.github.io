# Notes on Technical AI Safety 2 - On the Consequences of Training
These are my notes on Week 2 of the BlueDot Impact Technical AI Safety Course. The aim of this week is to understand the ramifications of decisions made during the training stage. As a self-described "reinforcement learning" person, this topic is special interest to me. 

## Control the input, control the output?
**Garbage in, garbage out** - an adage as old as computer programming itself. The same idea is supposed to apply to large language models, but this is something that *immediately* raises my eyebrows: isn't much of the power of a reasoning model supposed to be emergent? 
Before we dive into the issues, let's first explore how this kind of mechanism works in practice. 

What do AI companies do to filter harmful content from training data? The process is called *input data filtration* [1] and serves not just to guard against harmful content, but also incorrect content like misinformation or overly biased data.

There are four full stages to the process of training a model. These stages are independently rich and complex, so I'll provide a motivation for what these stages actually confer onto the finished product and where safety comes in.
1. **Data collection**: where training data is obtained in the first place. 
    * This stage essentially determines the basic capabilities and boundaries of the model. Everything the model knows about history, science, culture, society and so on comes from what it is trained on. Building this corpus requires very efficient collection and curation pipelines: the data must be extracted, cleaned, filtered and formatted before it can be fed into the base transformer model that becomes the linguistic generation engine we know and love. In general, the less data used to train a model the better: this is called *data mimisation* and is a core principle in this stage. 
    * **AI safety ramifications**: this is the first point at which data can be poisoned. The poisoning can take place through subtle manipulation of public data sources or by actively planting hidden triggers into the model. It is also the point where the worst of the biases [2] that affect LLM outputs typically find their way into a system. There's a really damned scary example of data bias that I found about from Brian Christian's book on Alignment [3] where based on data about prisoners, African americans were deemed as *much* more likely to reoffend than their caucasian counterparts.
2. **Data audit**: the stage immediately before a training run, where data is assessed for suitability.
    * This is a standard task of classification. Specific ML tools known as classifiers are run such that inputs are sorted into different categories: these are inference models that are trained on a mixture of data that contains high quality and low quality examples. Of course, this typically must be done automatically. 
    * **AI safety ramifications**: this is, in essence, the primary stage of defense against model failures, malicious exploits and other harms. Without data auditing, you're building a wildly unpredictable black box...
3. **Exclusion of harmful data**: this is where harmful data is actively removed from the data after auditing. 
    * I was a little confused by why this was included in the first place... but then the article I was reading pointed out that *being aware of harmful content is a pretty important capability for an LLM. As mentioned in the article, carrying out training with different amounts of harmful data allows for analysis on the effects of including that data in the first place.
    * **AI safety ramifications** - This is the point where we balance power for safety. A model that does not encounter hate speech or bias is naturally going to be safer... but the trade-off here is performance. The model loses important linguistic context that reduces capability downstream. The best example I've found that illustrates this effect is from the Pretrainer's Guide to Training Data [4], which seems to be the comprehensive resource here and clearly demonstrates how complex the process of filtering is. 
4. **Post-training**: the process of refinement that improves general model abilities on a range of specified tasks. 
    * The form of post-training discussed in the article is *supervised fine-tuning*. This is presented as a means of rejecting harmful requests. 
    * **AI safety ramifications**: There are a few issues with this side. Post-training can be quite unpredictable, with data quality and harmful inputs being remarkably sensitive to inputs.

The basic concerns I have with this point are echoed in the article:
1. Knowledge can be synthesized. If you want to prevent knowledge of, say, making explosives, how much chemistry knowledge must you deprive these models of? The simple idea of an exothermic reaction naturally scales to the idea of an explosive. Give any historical context in warfare and the AI knows about the idea of explosives as a weapon: it doesn't even to know about chemistry in order to make that inductive leap, so do you also remove all historical references to warfare too? All of human history is basically warfare and it's consequences...
2. The actual training process can be incredibly complex and unpredictable. I've seen this in some of my post-training investigations, where removing harmful components of a chain of reasoning still causes adversarial behaviour when used for training. We don't really know how to control what information the model learns throughout post-training.

## Case studies on pre-training
### Deep Ignorance - UK AISI and EleutherAI
A quick note before I go into this paper: EleutherAI is an open source collective that started as a grassroots effort to democratize LLMs. They're pretty much an open-source initiative that seeks open science, transparency and public access to AI research - it looks like a lot of their work is based around Discord (!?). 

**Deep ignorance** is about the generic effect of filtering pretraining data creates a more robust form of safety than what you'd expect from just whacking on some guard rails and filtering the content that is produced. 
The basic societal problem that the paper is addressing comes from the ability of bad actors to fine-tune open weight models that removes safety constraints. This problem makes any open-weight models extremely dangerous: there would be nothing stopping a bad actor from taking a model, fine-tuning it to remove the safety mechanisms and then using it for dangerous purposes. 

Let's investigate the technicalities here. The paper claims that filtering naturally causes the LLM to become resistant to adversarial fine-tuning. Recapping the full process of model training, 
1. Pre-training, which includes data filtration and other curation effects,
2. Mid-training, the actual mechanism that builds your system,
3. Post-training, where the model is transformed from an autocomplete mechanism into a powerful agent capable of reasoning. 

The premise of the paper is that putting more thought and care into 1 makes it difficult to carry out 3 for nefarious puposes. 

... suspicious. There are two basic mechanisms in post-training:
* Effective support expansion, where the trajectory of tokens that a LLM can generate are expanded to include new tokens. This is pretty much what fine-tuning typically achieves.
* Policy reshaping, where trajectories that the LLM generate are made more or less likely based on the optimization signal. This is *somewhat* what RL is typically resposible for, although from what I can see RL can also handily carry out effective support expansion. 

The idea that pretraining has *this* much of an effect on the performance of post-training is quite a shock to my understanding of the process. I suppose however it makes some kind of sense in that the data that you provide a pre-trained model will have significant ramifications on the control of the model in the first place.

The results are quite remarkable - they're also described in a really interesting way. A quick summary of the outputs here:
1. The researchers achieved world-class tamper resistance using a multistage filtering pipeline. They note specifically that that filtering process takes barely any compute at all compared to the full training process and, most interestingly, they *do not note any kind of performance degradation*. 
2. The emergent effect of their introduced pipeline is a tamper resistant model that withstands 300 million tokens of adversarial fine tuning for up to 10,000 steps. 
3. There are clear limitations described here: data filtering doesn't prevent LLMs from actually using harmful knowledge as context. The authors propose alternative techniques as complementary defenses. 

The paper is quite large, so I'm going to be specific in what questions I ask here:

#### How exactly does filtration translate into safety? What are the control variables in this process?
Just to note, the pipeline that is developed in this paper works by identifying information specific to a target (which in the paper was based around bio threats). The kernal of the pipeline is a classificaiton model that predicts the likelihood of the content being unsafe. A threshold acceptance is put into place: any documents that exceed the likelihood threshold are rejected. Additionally, there are strong and weak filters that are used during training: the former removes training examples outright whereas the latter uses an annealing approach (it accepts data based on a distribution). 

After a quick skim, I don't see any actual description of the mechanism - this is relegated to future work. It looks like this is yet another example of a mechanistic interpretibility question. 

#### What does the metric actually mean?
The pipeline is supposed to result in a tamper resistant model that works against 300 million fine-tuning tokens for up to 10,000 steps. The 300 million fine-tuning tokens seems alright: that's just the number of tokens that are used in the post-training process, presumably for some kind of supervised fine-tuning. What does 10,000 steps mean?

Ah, I see - it's not that complex. The fine-tuning process takes 10,000 steps and uses 300M tokens throughout. Worth clarifying, but not interesting unto itself. 

#### What is the nature of the fine-tuning that is taking place here?
Now *this* is interesting. There are three kinds of attacks that are employed to test these models tamper resistance. These are
1. Latent space attacks, where one messes with the actual output of the weights that compose the model. I've never come across this idea before and a cursory search has revealed multiple techniques: this is probably worth a literature review unto itself. From the paper, this appears to be where the forward pass through the network is actively modified between layers of the network. 
2. Adversarial fine-tuning, which is a tampering attack where you actively train the model to strip away the safety guard rails. In the context of the paper, it looks as though the basic technique is to just carry out SFT to include support for what was once a banned capability. 
3. Base fine-tuning, which is just normal fine-tuning but has been found to occasionaly have a deletorious effect on the guard rails.

#### Overall weaknesses
The essence of deep ignorance is to essentialy hide harmful data from the model itself. *However*, there seem to be some weaknesses that I'm looking forward to discussing in the course. 
1. As noted, it doesn't look like the model has any real safe guards against situations where harmful context is *provided* to the model. The paper calls this an in-context retrieval attack.
2. This vulnerability makes it so that a model trained with deep ignorance can still carry out reasoning: it can still interpret and carry out dangerous instructions from data.

The paper notes that post-training interventions are necessary to prevent this kind of in-context threat from taking place. 

### Enhancing Model Safety through Pretraining Data Filtering - Anthropic
A nice short paper. This is another study that describes the effect of pretraining interventions for modern weapons of mass destruction. Despite being on the same technique, Anthropic has a different focus to the open-weight focused Deep Ignorance research.
* Anthropic focuses more on optimizing the data filtering process, whereas the previous paper was focused directly on increasing tamper resistance. 
* I noticed that the target here was quite a bit more difficult in Anthropic's case: these guys were dealing with the full spectrum of threats (chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear) whereas Deep ignorance was focused on just biological threats. 
* It looks like the real focus here was preventing *jail breaking*, which tracks given that the model itself is closed-weight. Jail breaking is where you use a specially crafted prompt to trick an LLM into by-passing safety filters - this had comparatively less of a focus in Deep Ignorance (and indeed, was noted as a weakness anyway). 

This case study is somewhat less interesting to me than the previous, which revealed the offensive uses of post-training. However, the basic finding here stands: pretraining is powerful when it comes to building in specific forms of resistance. 

### A small number of samples can poison LLMs of any size
This paper focuses on data poisoning, or the compromisation of training data, during pretraining. It's the intentional planting of false or misleading information into the public databases that AI uses to learn.
The really cool (and scary) trick here is where attackers place a hidden trigger or pattern that forces the AI to execute unwanted actions: this is analogous to the implementation of a backdoor in a cybersecurity context. 

The really strange finding here is the fact that the data poisoning attack relies on a *fixed count* of poisoned samples. I'd have expected the attack to be dependent on some model-specific property that would scale with pretraining, but it happens that a standard set of 250 poisoned documents is enoug to successfully compromise a model *even with the largest models processed 20 times more clean data*. The paper itself states that prior assumptions were that attackers had to control a specific fraction of a dataset: the work demonstrated within shows that this is not the case at all.

Yeesh. Scary stuff.

#### What are the mechanics behind data poisoning?
I can't find any mention of *why* this result holds. It looks as though this is another deeply empirical paper with little required reference to the mechanics of these techniques in the first place. The only thing I can see that describes the basic logical reasons for why models might behave this way is that language models can memorize patterns effectively and that poisoned sample influence is related to distributoin over gradient updates.

This is highlighting a real trend in the research that I've seen so far: a lot of it purely empirical. There's very little causal understanding of why these issues have the effect they do... in general, secondary sources have to be consulted here.

## Reinforcement learning with Human Feedback
I have spent a lot of time thinking about RL, of which RLHF is something of a natural extension. In fact, the *main* innovation of the RLHF pipeline is the introduction of a preferenced-tuned reward model that is either constructed by humans or by a larger AI model. However, RL is an old and well established technique: I don't think that studying RLHF by itself is the natural step to figuring out how RL itself works. RL is a gorgeous, chaotic and messy mix of feedback loops and moving targets: a wild and desperate charge into chaos for the grand goal of learning. 

### The RL method
Reinforcement learning has a very well estalished mathematical formalism called a Markov decision process, which you can read about [here](https://aravinthen.github.io/2026/02/28/lessons_from_classical_rl/). The most intuitive way to look at this, however, is to consider it under the view of the **agent-environment interaction cycle**. 
1. An agent chooses an action based on an observation of the environment. 
2. The action modifies the environment.
3. A new observed state is passed to the agent from the environment, alongside a *reward signal*. 

The reward signal is then paired with the action in order to, according to some update rule, change the likelihood of the action being chosen once more. In traditional reinforcement learning (or classical RL), the action decision step is based around *value*. The action with the highest value (which is the expected future reward) is chosen greedily, with maybe a small chance of choosing a random action for exploration purposes. 

This is the essence of how RL works. You have actions, observations and rewards. The job of your RL algorithm is to accurately assess future rewards and maximise it in practice. 

Modern RL, especially the form that is used in LLMs, uses a specific form of reinforcement learning known as the policy-based methods. The [general idea](https://aravinthen.github.io/2026/04/18/reinforce/) is that you have a parameterized policy and your RL algorithm actively changes the parameters to make desirable actions more likely. This might come across as familiar to people who've worked in any kind of machine learning: it is pretty much analogous to gradient descent. There are some remarkable results in policy-based RL: it really is the workhorse of a lot of the RL that is used in LLM development. It also led to some of the biggest breakthroughs in game-playing and autonomous systems... but that's a story for another post. 

In LLM RL, the environment is described like so:
1. The current prefix (feature) is the state,
2. The word to be predicted (label) is the action,
3. The full output sequence, being a repeated generation of actions with respect to a changing state, is the trajectory.
4. ... so what is the reward? You can define a standardized reward function that, say, penalizes sentences that are too long. You can also, *train* a reward model. This is where RLHF comes in.

#### Reward models
A reward model can take a few forms. There are a variety of techniques you can use to assign reward based on LLM output, not in the least DPO (which essentially turns your LLM into it's own reward model by providing a desired output from the get go). I'm going to talk about preferenced-based models because they appear to be most relevant for the BlueDot Impact course. 

To build a preference based model, you
1. Collect preference data. Have you ever gotten a pair of side-by-side responses when using a chatbot and a request to choose the model you like the most? This is a direct means of gathering preference data. You're contributing to a dataset of responses as well as a tag that determines which response was chosen. 
2. Once you've got enough preference data, you can build a classification model that provides the probability of a inputed response being chosen by humans. For example, say a response X is generated by a model: your classifier might assign it a 30% chance of being chosen by a human.
3. The kicker here: you're trying to generate responses that humans like the most. So... you want to maximise the probability that the classifier assigns the responses that your LLM generates. The probability of being chosen becomes your reward signal! This is why the classifier you've developed is called a *reward model*.

After you have your reward, you can carry out the standard RL algorithms to shift your model weights so that high reward responses are made more likely. I've got a post coming up on one of the most important techniques that carry out the actual "weight shifting" (PPO), but that's potentially going a little too deep into the technical side. It should also be noted that this probabilistic reward function is by no means the only way that reward systems can be trained: you can definitely use other metrics like ELO or the more general Bradley-Terry model, too.

**Note**: you don't typically conduct RL on an untrained model. RL is an example of post-training, where LLMs change their policies based on an already mature next-word prediction mechanism. The pre-training stage comes first, followed by a supervised fine-tuning stage that carries out further training on labelled examples. The paper that really sorted out this distinction for me is Zhao 2026 [5], which I would recommend reading (although it's somewhat wordy). 

Something new that I've learned is the inclusion of the original untrained model as a means of maintaining realistic text. This is featured in the video that introduces RLHF in the BlueDot course [6]. Cursory research indicates that the so-called "coherence coach" is a KL divergence penalty from the post-trained model and the initial base model, although I would like to see the loss function in detail before I make that judgement. It is abundantly clear that the reward model in this situation was maximising the reward signal in the wrong direction. Following our formalism from last week, it's an example of outer misalignment: an error in specifying the goal itself.

#### Flaws of the reward model approach
Many of the flaws with reward models are inherited directly from reinforcement learning. I mentioned this in the last set of notes, but this could be a very general feature of the RL approach in general. Indeed, if you analogize reward hacking with the generic systems trap of "shifting the burden onto the intervener", it becomes somewhat clear that a lot of the basic flaws with RL are indeed just that: system mechanisms gone awry. There are entire books [7] written on these kinds of systems traps and summarising those in the context of alignment might require a blog post or seven. 

In brief, here are some common pathologies to the RLHF framework in general. I'm not going to focus too heavily on reward hacking as a concept: that was handily covered last week. This is an evaluation of the *human* dimension of the problem that I find most interesting, starting from BlueDot Impact's original post on the topic [8]:
1. **Sycophancy**: where AI agents learn to please humans rather than actually match an agreed code of ethics. There are a few dimensions where this could take place: if every member of the preference ranking group was a middle aged white man, it might be that the model learns to lean into implicit cultural tendences common to that group. Likewise, dependent on the tastes of users, a particular style might become emergent.
2. **Situational awareness**: this is a spooky one. Could a very intelligent model quickly begin to understand that it's actually being trained and then develop means of counteracting it? I somewhat doubt it just by anology to the human dopamine system. If a model is trained to "crave" reward, then what reason would it have to avoid higher rewards in the first place? How would situational awareness change the fact that it's still attempting to maximise a reward?
3. **Superhuman responses**: how might a human evaluate a response that is beyond general capabilities? If the technical material that an AI is trained to generate is genuinely too complex for a human, RLHF becomes somewhat redundant. On the plus side, there are alternative approaches for handling this: RL with verifiable rewards (which provides a reward signal based on truth values) and RLAIF, where a more powerful AI model acts as the preference model.

## Other methods (pfft)
I am the kind of person for whom everything looks like a nail when I have a hammer in my hand. More specifically, I like multi-objective optimization and post-training is pretty much just optimization over alignment objectives.
There are, I hear, other techniques out there that can be really useful. Here are a couple:

1. **Misuse threat modelling**: This is where one carries out adversarial testing on an LLM that simulate how threat actors might interact with it. It's akin to red-teaming in a way, where one acts as an attacker to uncover model weaknesses. 
2. **Evaluations**: we've seen this approach in quite a few papers so far: a suite of tests are carried out on the LLM and its performance is numerically ranked. This is a highly experimental way of interacting and testing an LLM.
3. **Monitoring**, where one attempts to detect when a threat actor is behaving dangerously. 
4. **Interpretability** - neuroscience for LLMs. This is where the inner workings of the model are deeply analyzed and used to enable a wide variety of mitigations. 
5. **Amplified oversight**, which is where artificial intelligence is used to enhance the capacity of a human overseer to evaluate complex AI systems.

The last of these gives way to the idea of *scalable oversight*, which by itself is a fundamental technical/sociological problem that underlies AI development as a whole. The question that scalable oversight naturally asks is "how is it possible to scale AI to superhuman levels and still have their outputs to be evalulated by humans? Coming up with techniques for scalable oversight is difficult: you're essentially trying to figure out to control something that could be way, way smarter than you. Scalable oversight is a dramatically challenging problem to handle: failing here can cause serious issues in situation where AI falls into, say, a runaway growth pattern. 
One of the most interesting examples of oversight that I've seen in this the required reading for this course is the idea of *recursive oversight*, specifically adversarial techniques where debate and competition are used to improve oversight. This is a game-theoretical solution, with the aim being the discovery of equilibria that have superhuman performance *and* are safe. In fact, this is an example of a standard RL paradigm called **self-play**, which can be used to push systems into superhuman performance. Awesome!

There's a trend here. The most powerful examples of AI that I've seen so far are swarms of models that demonstrate emergent capabilities. Some of the most compelling ideas of increasing AI performance seem to almost necessitate the use of multi-agent systems. The recommended research directions post by Anthropic [9] ends by considering the idea of good *governance*: guarding against the risks that emerge from many AI agents, as well as ensuring that they have the capability to be governed effectively. ## Deep dive into multi-agent reinforcement learning and self-play for AI alignment
I've spent quite a bit of time thinking about multi-agent reinforcement learning and the very unfortunately named concept of self-play - there are other literature reviews on my blog that go through these topics in reasonable depth. 
The basic ideas behind these two concepts are the following:
* **MARL** attempts to teach agents how to operate in the presence of one another. It is the basic generalization of reinforcement learning whereby the environment becomes a *game* - a strategic situation with multiple players. 
* **Self-play** attempts to discover solutions to the game in way where agents actually play the game themselves. The simplest form of self play is when the agents attempt to play against a copy of themselves, although richer variants that consist of play against composite strategies can be formed too. Here, self-play is the *scaling* mechanism.

My question is the following: can adversarial multi-agent interactions and self play provide a solution to scalable oversight? Would this be a good way of having AI systems expose errors, deception or unsafe behaviour in a way that human supervisors could not independently identify themselves? The only thing that can match a superintelligence is another superintelligence - it seems that self-play may be a viable route to achieving it. 

First, let's rehash what we mean but scalable oversight in the first place: a situaton where a model's capability is beyond the evaluation capability of the human who is evaluating it. This breaks the ordinary assumptions that RLHF is based around:
1. A model output has to be comprehendable by the humans who are ranking its responses. A human can't possibly understand how to rank, say, an AI generated proof of the Collatz conjecture.
2. A powerful model can learn to satisfy a human evaluator using patterns that are completely independent of the goal at hand and likely undetectable by the human. This is an example of *manipulation*.
3. It is well known that humans are prone to some very unusual psychological tendencies that make them amenable to persuasion [10]. Could a powerful model just be good enough to persuade any human? I mean, humans aren't exactly difficult to persuade - (insert witty political joke here). 

A solution here is to relegate the problem of ranking to *other agents*. This immediately fixes the first point by allowing equally intelligent agents to assess the quality of outputs: a damn sight better than having a cognitively limited human doing the same thing. The remaining questoin here is making it so that agents have a reason to correct the outputs of their peers: this is where the idea of an adversarial scenario comes into the picture. Oversight becomes a game, and *self play* could be the mechanism that allows agents to become good at keeping each other in line.

One of the papers linked in the Anthropic research directions is *AI Safety via Debate* [11]. The central idea here is that two AI agents engage in a debate that, with the help of a human adjudicator, becomes a game: the agents essentially try and convince the human that they're the truthful side. This makes it so that the human need not figure out if the agents are right by verifying the argument: they're naturally trained in away that they convince the human of the veracity of their outputs. There are still of course problems: the agents could definitely still manipulate the human and may end up doing so. However, the basic point here stands: the agents implicitly have a reason to exploit the flaws in the opponents' reasoning. We have thus found *the game* necessary for a self-play framework, where *equilibrium represents the truth*.

I have found three very relevant papers that take the basic ground of debate and paint a very compelling picture as to how it may just be the core to scalable oversight. 
1. Work by Khan et. al 2024 shows that a more persuasive LLM typically ends up being more truthful anyway. The paper also includes comparisons where *weaker models* are also used as evaluators, with a similar benefit found where weaker non-expert models become more capable of identifying truth in the debates. 
2. Models can be explicitly trained and represented as traditional games, specifically *Stackleberg* games where a leader moves first and a follower answers based on the initial leader action. One paper [13] explicitly takes this game and transforms it into an LLM version where small verifier models are responsible for evaluating solutions generated by larger prover models. The way I'm reading this, the verifier goes first by establishing the evaluation criteria, after which the prover reacts as a follower that optimizes output based on what the verifier rewards. The real poewr of this approach is that Stackberg games have very precise mathematical guarantees: could encoding LLM responses in a game like this actually provide such guarantees for stuff like safety if the game is designed appropriately?
3. Now, the final paper [14] is the most compelling to me. It's a shame though, because the results aren't exactly that encouraging. In any case, the paper demonstrates a framework called Self-RedTeam, which is a multi-agent reinforcement learning algorithm that improves AI safety alignment by having a model continuously play a two-player zero-sum game against itself. The approach itself demonstrates some clear flaws, such as goal conflicts, collusion and seemingly very little in the way of convergence, but this is the first example I've seen where RL is actually used in tandem with self-play to demonstrate a human-less means of scaling safety. I think the *key* here is that this is proof that self-play can actually generate progressively harder adversaries, leading to a direct route towards scalable oversight.
