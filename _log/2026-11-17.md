## BlueDot Technical AI Safety Project
1. The essential paper. This is what introduced the idea of AI debate in the first place.!
2. The core of the paper is something of a human feedback technique that employs self-play on a zero-sum debate game.
    1. Have two agents take turns making short statements up to a limit (already a constrained mechanism - what happens if the argument is so complex that it can't be summarized? 
    2. Human agents judge which of the agents gave the most true, useful information (again, another risk - what if human judges can't parse the complexity of the arguments?).
2. Ah, I see. The humans represent 'polynomial time' judges with limited time and resources. Without debate, the humans can only verify problems where the solution is checkable in polynomial time (NP-hard problems fall under this). *With* debate, judges can answer questions for a much larger class of problems (PSPACE), including multi-step and game-theoretic problems... typically much harder than NP.
3. *Strong* results just from the abstract. Damn, bloody good show.
4. I like how the paper specifies that alignment is a training time problem. I agree!
5. "Tasks difficult for a human to perform, but easy for the human to judge the correctness of". This breaks down when the tasks also become too complex for a human to judge, necessitating the use of AI judges. This is described later in the introduction, but instead of using a more capable judge the idea is to change the mechanism of information transfer in a way that makes it multi-step, presumably making it easier for agents to actually debate. 
6. "And flaws of flaws can have flaws" - banger line! In context, the idea here is to imagine a system capable of giving answers *and* pointing out flaws.
7. **Hypothesis**: optimal play in the debate game produces honest, aligned information far beyond the human judge. Further, optimal play emerges through self-play. I think self-play is the natural means of conducting this learning, given that the game itself is outlined as a zero-sum adversarial scenario.
8. "Debate is closely related to the amplification approach to AI alignment" - that is, starting with a human overseer and uses AI assistants to recursively break hard problems into smaller, verifiable pieces while keeping the system aligned.
9. The idea of mechanism design emerges almost naturally: in AlphaZero, the role of the human judge is akin to the *rules* of the game of Go.
10. Okay, so the debate protocol introduced here is an extensive form game where at first
    1. A question is shown to agents,
    2. Agents state their answers to the question,
    3. Agents make statements on their answers and their opponent answers,
    4. The judge sees the debate and decides the victor,
    5. Agents maximise probability of winning.
11. The **reward design** here is the protocol the human uses to decide which agent won the debate.
12. As expected, there are some claims that the work rests on, the first being that it is harder to lie than to refute a lie. This has precise ramifications for the debate equilibria...
13. Another potential mechanism: allow agents to switch. A basic extension: allow more than two agents.
14. The paper itself is quite long and demonstrates quite a few edge cases and interpretations. The smaller MNIST experiment is cool and has an interesting mechanism, but I think I've obtained the core of what this paper was trying to demonstrate.
