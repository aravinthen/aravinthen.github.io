## Reasoning
1. I've only looked into reinforcement learning with human feedback, but I recognise that this is something of an outdated approach to the problem of training a large language model. 
2. I'm reading *Direct Preference Optimization* by Cameron Wolfe. 
3. The impression that I'm getting here is that DPO is a supervised learning equivalent to RLHF, where training is carried out on the preference pairs directly: it provides a simpler objective that can be optimized with gradient descent. 
4. *Key idea*: DPO removes the need for a reference model. 
5. The essence of a **preference dataset** is one that includes 
    * The prompt, 
    * The accepted response, 
    * The rejected response.
6. This dataset can be converted into a reward form via the Bradley-Terry model, which is a generalization of ELO that predicts the likelihood of a response being chosen. 
7. Hmmmm. 
    * You know what this is kind of reminding me of? Food. Taste. I'm also quite hungry.
    * Say you have a food critic who is visiting your restaurant. In order to make sure that the food critic is satisfied, you hire an assistant whose entire job is to taste all the cakes that your chef makes and provide an accurate score relative to the critic. This is slow and inefficient, although having a really accurate representation of the food critic would be useful.
    * The alternative... is to provide the chef with direct comparisons. Say the chef has two recipes, A and B. She then consults the special book of comparisons that all chefs use for this particular critic (stretching it a bit here). She can use this book to pick the better recipe.
8. How does this work in practice?
    * Build the preference dataset. 
    * Load a pretrained model that remains frozen during training.
    * Load a policy model that starts with the same traits as the pre-trained model and updates during training.
    * For a given prompt, pass the preferred response and the not-preferred response to both models. 
    * Calculate the log-likelihoods of both responses for each model and then compute implicit rewards with the Bradley-Terry model: *the goal here is to measure how much the policy model favors each response relative to the reference model*. This is what drives the reward!
    * Calculate the binary cross-entropy loss, which reduces the probability of the not-preferred response (and increases for the preferred).
    * Update the weights of the policy using this loss.
9. As mentioned, this is something of a *supervised* approach to tuning a model based on preferences. I can see why the authors of Zhao et. al. indicated that DPO was a different class of technique... so why is it discussed as some kind of "better alternative" to RLHF?
10. *Ah*. No, I'm misremembering things. There are offline and on-line variants of preference optimization: I'm currently looking at the online version. As discussed by the original paper, both techniques are mostly for policy reshaping. 
11. A lot of modern agentic RL set-ups use PPO over DPO. It seems like RL is really, really good for carrying out exploration in complex multi-step scenarios, where finding behaviour that is difficult to rate via preference is the key.
