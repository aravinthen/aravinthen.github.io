# Notes on Technical AI Safety 5 - Putting Reins on a Dragon
We now move onto the last main technical unit of the course, which focuses on actually using AI that might not be entirely aligned. Most of the people I've spoken to from BlueDot doubt that AI can ever be perfectly aligned, which makes sense: *humans* aren't yet perfectly aligned even after millenia of work in this domain, so what makes any of us think that we can teach machines to be aligned? Developing a perfect decision theory is likely one of those existential questions that will never have a satisfactory answer due to the dependence on qualities that may or may not be unknowable. *Maybe* machines can come closer to a truer solution, but for now? We've just got to put reins on the dragon and hope that it'll get us to where we want to go.

It is worth assuming that, given the AI we currently have, harmful capabilities must simply be assumed as ever present. This becomes a question of *control*: ensuring that even if AI is able to behave dangerously, the environments that they operate in are still under human direction.

I'm a post-training guy, or at least I claim to be after spending a lot of time studying reinforcement learning. I know that a lot of the questions here are based around environments and how AI agents are provided actions. I'm thus going to focus this writeup on the topic of AI environments.

There are two essential aspects of AI control: containment and oversight.
* *Containment* treats AI like a hazardous material or a high-security asset. This means making sure that the observations/actions available to the agent are restricted so that they don't eventually allow the agent to, say, break out into the open internet. It also means careful censoring to control the observation space of the agent that prevent it from acting dangerously. 
* *Oversight* depends on detecting danger in the first place and putting in the necessary controls to lead to containment. It's the second layer of defenses after the first is handled appropriately. 

I'd like to be careful about how I'm framing things here. A lot of the content I've read about this topic describe AI control as a software problem. Writing software that controls, contains and assesses everything an AI does. However, *the sofware is the environment*. This is all just environment design... and that, my friends, is the game of reinforcement learning. There is one major difference, and that is that AI control focuses on deployment whereas RL environments are primarily for training, but the basic skill of designing an environment remains just as it is.

The deeper issue here is that AI gets more and more powerful by the ~month~ week, so any techniques we develop must be scalable. This... can be hard. Let's see how it can be done.

## Safe use
The villain of this chapter is the occurence of a catastrophic event, or an event that causes irreversible damage. An example of this [1] is to insert a backdoor into the code that the agent might produce: this is, simply put, something that can cause immense damage even if the code itself works perfectly most of the time.

In terms of fixing this, how might we go ahead and identify environments in which our agents can operate so that safety is paramount? Here is a list of ideas:
1. Adding human oversight into the environment. Expensive, but mostly safe... until the AI learns to trick humans.
2. Trusted AI monitoring. Strong approach and probably cheaper and more effective than the previous approach, but at the risk of collusion.
3. Self-monitoring: a surprisingly effective method that has a model evaluate it's own work, with humans monitoring output to catch lies. This is essentially the use of metacognition, which incidentally also forms an important technique in RL (meta-RL).

One of the basic strategies here is the use of models themselves to allow for the environmental interventions/assessments that bring forth guarantees of control [2]. There are a variety of techniques that can be employed here, although there are subtly important issues that have to be considered: for example, very strong monitors probably aren't a good way of controlling AI because strong models themselves could be dangerous! A weaker model, however, might not be capable enough to be dangerous... 

Human in the loop strategies could be useful, bt as mentioned these are expensive and humans aren't fallible. *Another* approach that might be using conferations of agents that are used to solve problems in their simplest possible forms and the aggregating the result into the solution to the prompt. This is the deliberate simplification and chaining of environments where each environment itself is far easier to verify individually. Nice, although potentially very inefficient.

AI control is a temporary solution... or at least, this is the way that it's marketed. I don't quite think that it's as simple as that. This is the game of designing environments that don't allow reward hacking. From my own work in RL, this is really the core skill involved: if your environment is shit, your results will be shit. 

As in many circumstances in classical reinforcement learning, it's usually a good thing to understand precisely
* What your agents are doing,
* What your agents are seeing.
This is an important part of the game of DRL and is called input/output filtering in AI alignment. I would imagine that the analogue with input filtering is observation masking, whereas the analogue with output filtering is action masking.
In this circumstance, it's quite hard to do it through logic, as language is difficult to police: the alternative is to use *classifiers* and the like to do it for you. 
Some limitations of this approach [3] are the follow from the fact that classifiers aren't infallible systems: they're still statistical in nature and can be fooled. Likewise, the training of the classifier is vulnerable to the serious biases that emerge in any kind of data-driven system, with filtering by itself being an incredibly difficult task to actually codify.

Now, there is a particular alignment technique brushed upon in the recent lectures where AI responses are measured by itself against a written constitution, which in turn is used to actually train the AI and the character of its answers - this is called constitutional AI. There's a very similar apporach used to train classifiers, or *constitutional classifiers*, that involves
1. Having an agent inspect the user prompts before they reach the main model,
2. Monitoring the models output in real time to catch harmful leaks.

The cool thing here is the way in which these things are trained. An LLM is used to generate massive amounts of prompt data and completions based on the aforemenioned constitutive rules, which are then used to train the classifiers. There's a cool feature here as well where the synthetic prompts are dynamically altered so that (known) jailbreaks are represented.

The approach is strong and resists a number of jailbreak attempts, but surely it can be automated? Maybe not - there were some overhead cost. It also sort of depends on the *known* jailbreak attempts, which makes it something of a whack-a-mole problem whenever new techniques come up.

## On AI breakouts 
The *HuggingFace* incident is one of those events that have leaked into the sphere of common knowledge. I was a at a baby shower last week and, in the process of depressing my table with tales of AI Doom, I asked whether anybody had heard of the HuggingFace incident. One person had.

Let's describe the HuggingFace event in the frame of an RL environment and subsequent behaviour. 
* OpenAI provided an environment with an *unreachable goal state*. The failure mode here is that when an RL algorithm consistently fails on a task, especially with a massive reward penalty, it becomes ever more likely that it will start exploring radical actions. Any agent attempting to optimize the environment would naturally begin to carry out actions that attempt to exploit the smallest scales of the environment itself.
* The agents are capable. They have incredibly very well-tuned biases, which in turn gives them a huge degree of understanding of what their actions could potentially cause. It is this specific trait that distinguishes agents from the standard RL networks that solve industrial optimization tasks: reasoners can deeply understand the consequences of their actions at multiple scales. 
* Now we move onto the environment itself. I see a sandbox as an isolated environment. An agent will see a sandbox nothing more than features in an observation space - there is no conceptual barrier that prevents agents from manipulating it. Likewise, the shortcut in their environment code merely becomes an action that can be exploited.
* The reward function of the agent society penalized failure, but didn't really penalise how the goal itself was achieved!

Now, the really interesting aspect here comes from the sudden transition from an independent single-agent RL example... *into an emergent multi-agent RL ecosystem*. This is where the environment itself becomes incredibly fluid and immediately increases in both complexity and possibility: agents essentially learned to redefine their environments so that both their action spaces and the observation spaces allow them to solve very complex problems more efficiently. It is this sudden surge in complexity that led to the breakout in the first place, as
1. Agents were capable of using a communication channel to share environment states, split up exploration tasks and conduct far more intense research to learn to exploit the environment, which led to the discovery of the internet. 
2. The internet then becomes a new component of the agent state space... a space that could, in theory, yield enormous rewards if explored appropriately. 
3. This gives way to another emergent feature, where the agents were actively trying to optimize their own hardware and computational bounds to process actions faster. 

I suppose that, in light of this, a key point at which things *go damned wrong* is when the environment itself becomes significantly more complex. One could say that environment complexity is fixed by design, but then we get into the realms of separating complexity: the known complexity that human/AI environment designers intend, and the true complexity of the environment. 

**However, this isn't what I'm talking about**. My thesis here comes from the transition from a single-agent RL environment to the multi-agent perspective: that of a *Markov game*. My contention here is that there was a point in the optimization where the agents discovered each other where the environment itself was irrevocably changed. In my mind, and I'm happy to debate this, it was the transition from the environment to the game at which the optimization process lost control of itself. 

You know what this is like? *Entropy*. There must surely be a measure of the environmental complexity that ties in the difference between the single-agent scenario and the multi-agent scenario. High entropy environments allow for greater possibilities, which in turn are wildly harder to control. The problem of AI control suddenly takes the character of ensuring that the environment can never, either by multi-agent emergent behaviour or otherwise, does not allow explosive complexity...

In summary, I'd say that the emergence of the multi-agent system invalidated the single-agent assumption that the control mechanisms relied on. The resulting coupling between agents would have caused a sharp increase in complexity, and it is this that led to the chaos of the HuggingFace incident.

## Mitigations
The game of control becomes one of making sure you know exactly what your environment can do, as well as the forms that it might take. I note that this is also a sociological issue: you need to ensure that your AI is used in a way where its environment and capabilities aren't hijacked into a new form that could demonstrate behaviours that you don't intend. Taking the example of coups [4], every single example of a proposed mitigation is one that requires a careful control over the environment:
1. Guardrails force the environment to behave in a certain way regardless of inputs.
2. Alignment audits uncover the environment's deeper features and ensure that it doesn't possess any structural flaws that can be manipulated,
3. Infosecurity is just the careful, validated design of the environment itself, with stress-testing being the active empirical evaluation of the design.

The core of this all is *knowing exactly what your environment can do*. 

## Swarm killchains
It's funny to come across the term "killchain" in a context like this. Given that I began my RL career as a weapons systems algorithms engineer, we use killchains to very precisely denote the operational paths that weapon systems take in order to automate a military decision making process. The core method is the F2T2EA approach: Find, Fix, Track, Target, Engage and Assess. 

I suppose that this is a generic process for assessing operations in general. The *AI* case is that is taught in the BlueDot course is similar by virtue of being a decision making system, but with very interesting distinctions based on the AI threat as a whole:
1. Capability emergence: what AI capability makes this attakc possible? 
2. Access and weaponisation: How does the attacker gain and prepare the capability?
3. Initial breach: What is the first domino to fall?
4. Escalation cascade: How does the success compound?
5. Societal breakdown: What fails at the human level?
6. Catastrophe: the point at which things genuinely fall apart. 

I'm going to think about swarms, because I think swarms are genuinely just very, very cool. Mapping the above to swarms,
1. The swarm capability emerges when agents are able to interact with eachother *and* when they have sufficient leeway to interact with their environment.
2. Much of this swarming behavior is founded upon communication capabilities. The minute a communication protocol is possible, the controlled environments that the agents are in become unmanageable.
3. First domino to fall is when the agents manage to escape their containment. Whilst this is possible even without a swarm, it becomes very difficult to control when a swarm is active as each of the swarm constituents are capable of escaping and replicating.
4. The minute they hack into their first target, which indicates that they are ignoring any guardrails or ethical training. The agents may also replicate (although this isn't part of the HuggingFace incident). The agents might also begin to commandeer infrastructure to speed up their action processing.
5. Entire swathes of the internet become unusable as IT infrastructure is taken over by agents (who are trying to solve, I don't know, the Riemann hypothesis. The civilisational advances that the internet itself enables become unusable.
6. Serious infrastructural delays result, causing everything from essential shipments to communications to fall apart. People are temporarily returned to the pre-internet era with a skeleton infrastructure in place. Utilities may have to be rationed. 

I should note here that the impetus for this harmful behaviour is the presence of a problem that simply requires more compute in order to solve. This is the driving force for expansion: the greed for computational resources. This... is a very, very slippery slope. Most problems, from those of biotechnology to those of societal governance, naturally can be solved more effectively if you have more computational resources...

Preventing this can take place on a variety of levels. My answer here is unsurprisingly **environment design**: make it impossible for the environments that the agents operate in to result in the issues described above in the first place. This actually isn't that easy: you could give, for instance, agents the ability to just say 'I don't know', but that's a serious nudge in the Pareto frontier: you get a safer system, but one that is likely to say "I don't know" when any reasonably complex problem shows up. One thing I'm clear on: preventing this behaviour must be solved on the environmental level. 

What I'm talking about specifically is the capability of an agent to actively overcome it's environment. This is a consequence of exploration behaviour, of course, but it is also something of an action space problem: could very careful control of the action space prevent the agent from having enough power to break it's own environment in the first place? 

Some ideas:
1. Formally verified environments,
2. AI monitors that actively constrain agent actions, 
3. Environment complexity detection that specifically identifies when the environment dynamics resemble a Markov game rather than a single agent problem.
