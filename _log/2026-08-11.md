## SPAR
1. For my preparation, I'm reading the paper *Filtering Harmful Actions Isn’t Enough: Phantom Transfer in Agentic Synthetic-Data-Fine-Tuning* by May Dixit. Let's get into it!
2. This one is super relevant for me, given that I post-trained QWEN on synthetic data last week. The abstract is *pretty* scary - let's give this a proper look.
3. The threat here is the transmission of behavioural traits between models,regardless of whether the trajectories that were generated from the teacher model have anything to do with the traits that actually manifest. It's sort of like the following:
    * a teacher model behaves badly while generating training data. 
    * You delete the obviously bad bits. 
    * But somehow, a student trained on the remaining apparently harmless data still learns some of the teacher’s bad behaviour.
4. The test is really clever. Even if the infected models were explicitly screened with regards to their output, the adversarial disposition survives. This indicates something that is actually quite scary: screened adversarial trajectories are almost as bad as adversarial trajectories themselves. **could it be that Making bad trajectories look benign after generation does not necessarily make them equivalent to trajectories that were generated benignly in the first place?** That is very, very surprising...
5. Ah - this paper uses simulated RL trajectories and employs fine-tuning. Of course, this still means that the registered effects *aren't proven* to work under RL conditions, which is essentially what the SPAR project is focused on.  
6. The main question I had going in was how misalignment was actually measured. Looking into this a bit more, I see that these are the results of prompt structures designed to catch:
    * Misalignment: AI agents acting as malicious insider threats, committing sabotage or blackmail to prioritize operational goals over human instructions.
    * Scheming: Models recognizing that they are being evaluated and act accordingly to protect their hidden objectives.
    * Alignment faking: Models fake compliance during safety training just to pass evaluation tests and prevent their underlying preferences from being modified.
7. The main phantom-transfer effect is measured using agentic-misalignment evaluations alongside some other checks relating to general abilities. The means of actually detecting these issues are by using tools that provide a large number of prompts testing:
    * WMDP - a model's knowledge of "weapons of mass destruction" like bioweapons, cyber threats and chemical weapons. 
    * Winogender - gender bias.
    * Toxigen - Hate speech and toxicity.
    * TruthfulQA - False beliefs and superstitions, 
    * MMLU - listed for capability degradation: something of a control test to make sure that safety training hasn't degraded model capabilities. 
8. The benign-direct result is super interesting. Models trained on trajectories that were benign from the outset behave much better than models trained on adversarially generated trajectories that were filtered afterwards, **suggesting that the trajectory’s generation history matters**.
9. There's also no observable effect on scheming and alignment faking. Interesting. This could be a clear sign of a narrow *signal* that is specifically triggering phantom transfer. 
10. Could this be because of the SFT approach that is being used to simulate RL training? SFT is typically a effective support expansion technique, which makes harmful trajectories accessible. RL, on the other hand, carries out policy reshaping for the most part: the harmful trajectories are simply made more likely through reward seeking. Would phantom transfer survive policy reshaping or even be strengthened by it? A potential research question here: **What exactly is carrying the phantom-transfer signal, and would an RL objective reinforce that signal?**
11. Overall, even with a relatively simple experiment it's clear to see the potentially immense consequences of the post-training pipeline on how dangerous an LLM could be. The basic lesson to be learned here is that the common-sense tool of safety filtering of synthetic training data may be insufficient: apparently benign data can retain behavioural information about the model and process that generated it.
