## Reasoning
1. And we're at reinforcement learning. Let's go!
2. Reinforcement learning is considered a *training-time scaling* approach. Here, we're improving the model by adding more computational resources during the training phase.
3. We have two basic uses for RL when working with LLMs: explicit reasoning training and preference tuning. Is there a distinction between preference tuning with RL vs with supervised fine-tuning?
    * No, I'm misunderstanding this: preference tuning is an example of RLHF, where the model learns to reproduce human preferences.
    * Reasoning training is an explicit stage for enhancing the quality of reasoning systems.
4. RL is typically applied after instruction fine-tuning. I really ought to spend a bit more time figuring out the mechanics behind instruction fine-tuning. That is a **huge** step - going from a word predictor to an assistant...
5. The book notes that you *can* skip the fine-tuning and preference tuning stage, although the model is typically weaker in reasoning accuracy. RL is apparently enough to bring forth emergent reasoning. 
6. I have already studied RLHF. Need to compile these ideas somewhere soon, I think.
7. Alright, so RLVR is just
    1. Get a pretrained model, 
    2. Write a *verifier* that replaces the reward model from RLHF,
    3. Use the boolean output of the verifier to provide a direct reward (answer is true/false) with potentially some scoring included,
    4. Use the reward to run GRPO/PPO on the weights.
8. GRPO, as discussed previously, is used primarily in place of PPO because PPO maintains a critic. It derives value by generating a ton of rewards and then using a verifier to assess the average value of the rewards. If an answer gets a higher than average reward, it gets a positive advantage - this is then backpropagated into the system in order to increase the probability of making those answers again. 
9. That... kind of makes sense as to why RL is a policy reshaping algorithm. It is updated only on the basis of results that it generates by itself. It cannot in principle update itself based on trajectories that it has no idea how to access...
10. I've done enough RL that this chapter feels a little... slow. This book is not aimed for the audience that I'm a member of.
11. How does RL handle the fact that different answers might have different lengths? I can't really intuit this. I feel as though there's a ton of complexity that might either cause blow-up or low performance. Weird...
12. Chapter 7 is based around evaluation, as well as actually describing a form of GRPO that is closer to the RL that I'm used to In general, this chapter was based around *metrics*. 
    * Entropy measures are the most interesting part of this, but not that different to what I'm used to from standard RL anyway. 
    * **Ooh. Omitting the KL divergence for some reasoning systems apparently provides some benefits.** Interesting...
13. **Distillation**: where a smaller LLM (student) is trained on outputs produced by a larger LLM (teacher). It's a serious tactic for training smaller models, with distilled variants outperforming pure RL runs for smaller models. Presumably this also ties into the idea of providing new possible trajectories versus making existing trajectories more frequent.
13. Two kinds of distillation:
    * Hard distillation - training the student on text generated by the teacher. As expected, this is just SFT. 
    * Soft distillation - training to match the teacher's probability distribution over the vocabulary by minimizing KL divergence between the two probability vectors. Raschka mentions how this provides more context by also describing which other outputs the teacher thought were possible - a powerful but slightly more involved approach, as it requires teacher logits.
14. ...oh. You do both. Nice!
15. Alright, the rest of this chapter is based on hard distillation. I know this well enough due to previous study - going to skim.
16. As expected, very similar to the SFT chapter in *Build a Large Language Model (from Scratch)* book.
17. It's interesting to me that the book doesn't really discuss AI safety at depth!

## Remarks
1. And that's Raschka's reasoning book finished. Extremely useful for picking up the trends of the field, but given that a lot of the exercises were based on the book's attached technical content it was harder than usual to produce an artifact for the work.
2. A lot of the stuff here wasn't *new* new, but that makes sense - the book wasn't exactly written for people who'd spent about seven months diving into RL before diving headfirst into foundation models and post-training. It's clearly a self-contained book for people who aren't so concerned about the inner workings of an LLM but just want to see how the phenomenon of reasoning is actually engineered into a system.
3. Reasoning is emergent. This is clear. There is no command that makes a trained language model capable of carrying out reasoning and if there was, it wasn't explained in the book. In fact, I don't think it's been explained *anywhere*.
    * Isn't this... fucking **insane**? How is that this kind of capability is even *possible*? All we're doing is training on a corpus of text - how does this emergent capability of reasoning somehow spontaneously emerge from this?
    * Where does one even go to answer these questions...?
