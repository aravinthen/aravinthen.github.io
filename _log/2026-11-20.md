## BlueDot Technical AI Safety Project
1. Computational resources appear to be the death knell for most debate problems. I asked ChatGPT to recommend me a paper that handles this in terms of game theory: the closest result is this surprisingly modern paper here. It was only published two years ago!
2. The paper begins by noting that the original debate protocol was based around simulating deterministic systems for an exponential number of steps. This is computationally ridiculous and doesn't reflect the stochastic AI that we currently use. The example provided here is in checking legal contracts, where having a human expert to relay AI output would be prohibitively expensive in terms of time.
3. More connections between AI Debate, weak-to-strong generalization and computational complexity. I really ought to be reading more about computational complexity: it pops up literally everywhere...
3. Alright, so what I'm getting here is that double efficiency is when two polynomial-time provers compete to convince a more efficient verifier of the correctness of a computation. Indeed, this relaxes the efficiency constraints *significantly* from the original debate formulation by Amodei et. al. The verifier itself is actually a *constant time* function. That's seriously more applicable!
4. Alright, so the basic approach is that we have a machine that represents the reasoning trace and an oracle that represents a black-box human judgement. The debate works by *predicting* what the human oracle would say at each step to build their full reasoning trace.
5. ... holy shit. Brilliant, and very Google DeepMind coded!
6. The debate protocol here is pretty brilliant. You have two provers, A and B, as well as a verifier V.
    1. First, A presents a computation. 
    2. Second, B attempts to identifies an error.
    3. V checks the disputed step. It doesn't have to reproduce the entire computation. 
    4. V determines the winner. If the disputed step is correct, A wins. If it's incorrect, then B wins. 
7. The mechanism works by assuming that the generator (which is what the first prover is) must just have *one* invalid step. The goal of B is to simply find that step... and it has no incentive to not find that step, because if it reports a wrong result to V then it's lost the game!
8. The above is just one of the mechanisms described in the paper: deterministic cross-examination. The others are
    * Recursive debate, which is binary search for an error,
    * Stochastic debate, which is where probability estimates are directly challenged. This one is *complex*, and alas, I haven't the time to go through it properly.
    * Debate with a witness, where it seems like the agents propose and defend a solution.

## AI safety
1. `HIVEMARKET` and the ideas that are emerging around it are based heavily on the idea of multi-agent systems. Specifically, swarms. 
2. The complexity of swarms comes mostly from the fact that you have the constituent members of the swarm, as well as the swarm itself as an entity. The analogies here come from thermodynamics and analytical mechanics. Where thermodynamicists consider the 'swarm', whereas mechanics - the actual 'laws' that govern how the 'members of the swarm' operate. It is the aggregate of the latter that determines the former.
3. My collaborators had a great discussion on the components of our project. We've somewhat moved into three streams:
    * Capabilities, where we provide mechanisms to the swarms that allow them to solve complex problems.
    * Misalignment, where the capabilities determined previously are torn to shreds and dangerous behaviour is elicited instead.
    * Interpretation: the behavior of the constituent agents that actually caused the misalignment. 
4. I feel as though this is a general enough framework to allow for some really cool investigation. We're collaborating on a `LessWrong` post and will have that submitted sooner rather than later.
